Abstract.The galaxy bispectrum directly probes the non-linear gravitational evolu-
tion of large-scale structure (LSS) and can break parameter degeneracies that remain
in power-spectrum analyses. We present a joint full-shape cosmological analysis of
three luminous red galaxy (LRG) redshift bins and the quasar (QSO) sample from
the first Data Release (DR1) of the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI).
We model the redshift-space power spectrum at one loop and the tree-level bispec-
trum within the Effective Field Theory of LSS. The bispectrum is decomposed in the
Tripolar Spherical Harmonics basis, for which the convolution with the survey window
function can be formulated as a direct linear transformation of the theoretical multi-
poles. Our power-spectrum constraints are in good agreement with the official DESI
DR1 full-modelling results. We investigate the impact of including the bispectrum

in the inference, finding that the monopole substantially improves the constraints on
the cold dark matter density and amplitude of matter fluctuations by 9-18% and 8-
20%, respectively, in the individual-tracer analyses. The corresponding reductions are
15% and 10% for the combined LRG sample, and 6% and 4% when all tracers are
combined. In a restricted test using the first LRG bin, the bispectrum quadrupole
changes the marginalised uncertainties by only a few percent. Extending the analysis
tow 0waCDM substantially broadens the cosmological posteriors, while the bispectrum
produces only a mild change in the allowed dark-energy parameter region, which re-
mains sensitive to the adopted prior ranges. Our results demonstrate the potential of
higher-order clustering statistics to improve cosmological constraints, while providing
a framework for incorporating the bispectrum into full-shape analyses of current and
future spectroscopic galaxy surveys.

1 Introduction
Galaxy redshift surveys measure the three-dimensional distribution of biased tracers
and provide one of the main probes of cosmic expansion and the growth of structure.
While baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) measurements provide a robust standard
ruler, full-shape analyses use the scale dependence of the clustering power spectrum
and its redshift-space anisotropies to extract additional information on the matter
density, the amplitude of fluctuations, the growth rate, and galaxy bias [1, 2]. Much of
this additional information can be obtained from the scales where the density field is
no longer in the linear regime, making an accurate description of non-linear structure
formation essential.
Non-linear gravitational collapse of matter is responsible for the interaction of
different scales, which in Fourier space is manifested through the coupling of different
modesk i. At late times, these interactions generate non-negligible deviations from
the nearly Gaussian statistics expected from the linear evolution of the initial condi-
tions [3]. Quantifying the agreement of such gravitationally induced non-Gaussianities
with theoretical predictions is essential for testing the assumptions and validity of our
cosmological models.
We can study how these non-Gaussianities are generated analytically by describing
the non-linear gravitational evolution through a convolution ofnFourier modes of the
initial (linear) density fieldδ (1), weighted by a kernelF n that describes the mode
coupling induced by gravity at ordernin standard perturbation theory (SPT) [4–6].
With the two-point function in Fourier space (namely, the power spectrum), 1
⟨δ(k1)δ(k2)⟩= (2π) 3δD(k1 +k 2)P(k 1),(1.1)
deviations from the linear prediction are already present due to loop corrections that
come from the higher-order terms in the SPT expansion, such asδ(2) andδ (3). However,
since a Gaussian random field is fully characterised by its two-point function, with all
connected higher-order moments vanishing identically [7], the three-point function, or
bispectrum in Fourier space,
⟨δ(k1)δ(k2)δ(k3)⟩= (2π) 3δD(k1 +k 2 +k 3)B(k 1,k 2,k 3),(1.2)
is the lowest-order statistic sensitive to the non-Gaussian features, thus providing a
direct probe of the non-linear gravitational interactions responsible for mode coupling
in the late-time matter distribution. Therefore, in this work we consider a joint analysis
of the galaxy bispectrumB(k 1,k 2,k 3) and power spectrumP(k 1).
In addition to providing us with a better description of the distribution of galaxies,
the bispectrum also helps us to constrain the galaxy bias expansion, which captures
the galaxy formation process by a set of unknown bias parameters. Because of that, we
are able to constrain non-linear bias contributions that lift degeneracies present in the
linear theory, such as the linear bias and amplitude degeneracy in the power spectrum
[8, 9], and high-order biases [10, 11]. This is one of the main motivations for joint

power-spectrum and bispectrum analyses in perturbative models of galaxy clustering

One of the main goals of modern full-shape analyses of the power spectrum and
bispectrum is to extend the modelling to the quasi-linear regime, while maintaining an
accuracy compatible with the statistical precision of current galaxy redshift surveys.
These scales contain a large fraction of the observable Fourier modes, which reduces the
statistical uncertainties due to an improved sampling of the underlying density field.
The primary advantage of current spectroscopic surveys, such as the Dark Energy
Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), is their large effective volume and broad redshift
coverage, which reduce sample variance and provide several galaxy and quasar samples
with complementary bias and redshift dependence. The number density of each sample
then controls the balance between shot noise and sample variance on the scales included
in the analysis [2, 15, 16].
However, the accurate modelling of the quasi-linear regime remains challenging.
The loop corrections arising in the SPT receive contributions from small, non-linear
scales where the perturbation expansion breaks down. The Effective Field Theory
(EFT) of Large-Scale Structure (LSS) provides a physically motivated way of account-
ing for their impact on long-wavelength modes: unresolved short-distance dynamics
is absorbed into counterterms and stochastic contributions consistent with the sym-
metries of the problem, making the perturbative description predictive on mildly non-
linear scales [17–22].
Our primary goal in this work is to constrain the ΛCDM model and the galaxy
bias parameters by conducting the first full-shape analysis of the joint galaxy power
spectrum and bispectrum with DESI Year 1 data [16], accounting for the effects of
survey geometry in a consistent way via the Tripolar Spherical Harmonics (TripoSH)
decomposition of the redshift-space bispectrum, and within the framework of EFT.
We also consider the extension to a time-dependent dark-energy equation of state
described by the Chevallier-Polarski-Linder (CPL) parametrisation,w(a) =w 0+wa(1−
a) [23, 24].
We structure this manuscript as follows. In Section 2, we describe our modelling
choice, which is based on the EFT of LSS, for both the power spectrum and bispectrum.
We also discuss the TripoSH decomposition in Section 2.2.1, and we give a general
overview of the window function convolution in Section 2.4, leaving the specific details
of the DESI DR1 power spectrum window treatment to Section 3.1. We provide a
full description of the bispectrum window function in Appendix A. Sections 3 and
4 introduce the main data catalogues and simulations employed in our analysis. In
Section 5, we present the methodology chosen: the Bayesian inference formalism, the
likelihood and priors assumed, and the sampler and emulators chosen. Finally, the
results are presented in Section 6, and we conclude in Section 7. The validity of the
pipeline, tested againstN-body simulations, is left for Appendix B.

2 Modelling
The statistical power of galaxy surveys increases rapidly as smaller scales are included,
since the number of independent Fourier modes grows approximately ask 2 dk. The
challenge is that these same scales are progressively affected by non-linear gravitational
evolution, galaxy bias, and redshift-space distortions. Standard perturbation theory
is controlled only while the long-wavelength density fluctuations remain small, and it
breaks down as the wavenumber approaches the non-linear scalek NL, defined schemat-
ically byδ(k NL)∼ O(1). A central goal of modern large-scale-structure modelling is
therefore to extend the range of scales that can be used in a controlled way, without
attempting to describe the fully non-linear short-distance dynamics explicitly.
The EFT of LSS provides a systematic framework for this purpose. In this context,
EFT means a long-wavelength description obtained after coarse-graining over short-
scale modes. The long-wavelength dark-matter fields are described as an effective fluid
coupled to gravity, while the impact of unresolved short-distance physics is absorbed
into counterterms and stochastic contributions that supplement the perturbative ex-
pansion with all counterterms allowed by the symmetries of the problem: equivalence
principle, rotational invariance, and mass and momentum conservation [17–22, 25]; see
[26, 27] for reviews. This makes the perturbative description renormalised and predic-
tive on mildly non-linear scales, with an accuracy controlled by ratios such ask/k NL
and by the perturbative order used in the calculation.
In this work, we use this framework for the redshift-space galaxy power spectrum
at one loop and for the galaxy bispectrum at tree level. Our implementation follows
theFOLPSperturbative solver [28–30], which provides fast loop calculations and can
include kernels beyond the Einstein–de Sitter approximation when required. Infrared
(IR) resummation is used to account for the large-scale bulk displacements that smooth
the baryon acoustic oscillation feature [31–33].
For biased tracers, the small-scale physics of galaxy formation is absorbed into a
set of bias coefficients multiplying all operators allowed by the symmetries [20, 25, 26,
34, 35]. At fixed timeτ, the galaxy overdensity in real space can be written as

4 Simulations
4.1N-body simulations
We consider theAbacusSummitsimulation suite [61] and adopt the baseline ΛCDM
cosmology as the reference case for consistency checks. The parameters are set to
the mean of the full temperature, polarisation, and lensing (TT, TE, EE + lowE +
lensing) data obtained from the Planck 2018Plikresults [62]:ω b = 0.02237,ω cdm =
0.12,h= 0.6736,A s = 2.0830×10 −9,n s = 0.9649,N ur = 2.0328,N ncdm = 1,
ωncdm = 6.442×10 −4.
We use 25 independent realisations of this baseline cosmology generated in boxes
of sideL= 2 Gpc/h, with a particle mass of 2.1×10 9 M⊙/h. These simulations were
built to generate mock catalogues reproducing the clustering of many DESI tracers,
but we will only consider LRGs and QSOs: for the former, 50 snapshots have been
generated (25 atz= 0.5, 25 atz= 0.8), while for the latter, we have 25 snapshots at
z= 1.4.
From these snapshots, cubic mock catalogues were created to replicate the cluster-
ing of LRGs at redshiftsz= 0.5 (LRG1),z= 0.8 (LRG2) andz= 1.1 (LRG3) and of
QSOs atz= 1.4. These are part of the so-called “Second Generation” of mocks, which
were created by populating the dark matter halos with the halo occupation distribution
(HOD) model of [63], fitted to the final DESI early data release [64]. The final periodic
mocks reproduced the clustering of LRGs at 0.4< z <0.6 (LRG1), 0.6< z <0.8
(LRG2), and 0.8< z <1.1 (LRG3), and quasars (QSO) between 0.8< z <2.1. When
constructing the redshift-space catalogues, the HOD accounted for an incompleteness
factor 0< f ic ≤1, and velocity-bias parameters for central and satellite galaxies [65].
The number densities, in units ofh 3/Mpc3, are ¯nLRG1 = 0.00070, ¯nLRG2 = 0.00085,
¯nLRG3 = 0.00081, and ¯nQSO = 0.00017. In what follows, we refer to these cubic mocks
simply as “boxes”.
Finally, two other sets of mocks were created, in order to reproduce the actual
DESI observations: the “complete” and “alternate Merged Target Ledgers” (altMTL)
mocks. From boxes described above, the complete catalogues were created by convert-
ing the Cartesian coordinates into the corresponding right ascension, declination and
“observed” redshifts (we only consider those in redshift space), followed by the DESI
footprint “cut”: the angular positions were selected according to the area of obser-
vations, and the radial selection was applied in order to match the observed number
densityn(z) of tracers. Because of that, we stress that these mocks are not proper
light cones, in the sense that they do not carry any redshift evolution of the tracers.
Finally, by applying fibre assignment of DESI observations to the complete mocks, the
altMTL catalogues were generated with the same incompleteness as the real data, due
to the fibre collisions.
Figure 3 shows an example of the power spectrum and bispectrum multipoles of
these different sets of mocks for the second LRG bin (0.6< z <0.8) in the SGC
region. From these mock measurements, we can assess the impact of fibre collisions
on the different statistics: it is most evident in the small-scale amplitude of the power
spectrum monopole and the bispectrum. The “complete” case (dotted), which does

4.2 Covariance
The covariance matrixCis estimated from 1000 realisations of a periodic box of side
Lbox = 6 Gpc/h, obtained via theEZmockscode [66]. This code employs the Zel’dovich
approximation to generate the dark matter density field, with an effective bias to
describe the stochastic, non-local and non-linear contributions. These are calibrated
to theAbacusSummitmocks to describe the galaxy field of interest.
In order to obtain realistic covariance matrices, the EZmocks were post-processed
with an emulator that mimics the effect of fibre collisions: because applying the DESI
fibre assignment algorithm to the 1000 mocks for each tracer and redshift bin was very
time consuming, there are no “altMTL” catalogues for the EZmocks used to construct
the covariance matrices. Instead, the incomplete set of EZmocks has been generated
with the “fast-fibre assignment” (FFA) emulator described in [67].
As shown in Figure 3, the FFA algorithm does not capture the effects of the
realistic altMTL fibre assignment. However, the variances are all consistent if one
accounts for fibre assignment. Therefore, for the purposes of our analysis, we use the
covariances obtained from the 1000 FFA EZmocks.

We sample our posterior distribution with thepocoMCsampler [75]: it implements the

posterior distribution in the parameter spaceθinto a new coordinate systemu=f −1(θ)
in which the posterior geometry is simplified (more Gaussian and less correlated),
making the sampling more efficient [76].

5.2 Emulators
A major computational bottleneck in modern cosmological inference is the repeated
evaluation of accurate but expensive forward models within likelihood and sampling
pipelines. Emulators address this problem by learning a fast surrogate of the target
observable from a training set of high-fidelity calculations, thereby enabling orders-of-
magnitude speed-ups while retaining sufficient accuracy for parameter estimation. This
approach is now widely used in cosmology, from early CMB and likelihood emulation
to recent emulators for cosmological power spectra, galaxy clustering, and EFT-based
large-scale-structure predictions [77–83].
For all of our ΛCDM analyses, we compute the linear matter power spectrum
using thebaccoemuemulator [80], which predicts the linear power spectrum with sub-
percent accuracy over the scales relevant for LSS analyses.
To explore models beyond ΛCDM, in particular thew 0wa analysis presented
in Section 6.3, we employ instead theMapse.jlemulator, as it was trained on a
larger parameter space thanbaccoemu. It belongs to the same family asCapse.jl
andEffort.jl, sharing the neural-network infrastructure implemented inAbstract-
CosmologicalEmulators.jl[79, 83, 84]. In this work we use its JAX implementation,
jaxmapse,10 which provides the version adopted in ourw 0wa analysis. To reach an ac-
curacy of about 0.1% over a widew 0waMνCDM parameter space,Mapse.jlemulates
only the transfer function, treats ln(1010As) andn s analytically, factors out the leading
redshift dependence through division byD 2(z), removes additional smooth structure
through a symbolic approximation obtained by genetic programming, and finally com-
presses the target space via principal component analysis before the regression stage
[84]. By doing this,jaxmapsereaches submillisecond performance when computing
the linear power spectrum for a single redshift.
As a consistency test, we repeated the same ΛCDM inference withjaxmapseand
baccoemuemulators. The shifts in the posterior centres are much smaller than the
corresponding 1-σuncertainties for all parameters. The posterior widths also agree at
the few-percent level, with the largest difference being an approximately 6% tighter
constraint onω cdm, when usingbaccoemu. At the precision of the present analysis, the
two implementations therefore lead to statistically consistent cosmological constraints.

6.1 Power-spectrum-only analysis
Before we discuss the role of the bispectrum monopole in the analysis, we start by
validating the statistical consistency of our power-spectrum-only analyses (P0+P2) with
the DESI DR1 “full-modelling” results for the same individual tracers [2]. Since both
analyses use the same measurements and covariance matrices, the posterior estimates
can be correlated, so we do not assign a formal significance to shifts in the posteriors.
Instead, we compare the constraints directly (see Table 4 for numerical results), finding
good agreement for all four tracers.
The largest differences with respect to the DESI 2024 power-spectrum-only con-
straints are observed for the LRG1 and QSO samples, where the posterior centres of
ωcdm are shifted to higher values. In Figure 4, we compare the DESI 2024 full-modelling
results with our reference choice of reparametrisation and priors (see Table 2), but we
also show results obtained by removing theA AP scaling and reparametrising only the
biases withσ 8, as done in [2] (“DESI repar.” in the figure), first retaining our broad
power-spectrum counterterms priors and then imposing the tighter DESI priors (“full
DESI priors” in the figure). As we show in Appendix B, under the ΛCDM model our
reparametrisation affectsω cdm more strongly than the other cosmological parameters
and can broaden its marginalised posterior. Tests on the mean mock measurements
nevertheless recover the fiducial cosmology within the expected uncertainties. The dis-
placement observed in the data should therefore be interpreted as the combined effect
of the realised data vector and the nuisance-parameter prior geometry, rather than as
evidence for a systematic bias of the pipeline.
For the DR1 LRG1 bin, we find the 1-σuncertainty on Ωm to beσ≃0.025 for the
reference analysis andσ≃0.023 when adopting the full DESI priors. These correspond
to uncertainties that are 22% and 10% larger than those of [2], respectively. For the
QSOs, we obtainedσ≃0.047 (reference),σ≃0.039 (full DESI priors), larger than
the results in [2] by 31% and 9%, respectively. We find the width of theω cdm posterior
to be affected by the prior adopted for the power-spectrum monopolec 0 counterterm:
broader priors lead to broader posteriors forω cdm, while the remaining cosmological
parameters are only weakly affected. Broader priors onc 0 therefore allowω cdm greater
freedom to shift towards higher values.

7 Conclusions
In this work, we have presented a joint full-shape analysis of the DESI DR1 power spec-
trum and bispectrum for three LRG redshift bins and the QSO sample. We combined
a one-loop EFT description of the power spectrum with the tree-level bispectrum,
decomposed in the TripoSH basis, and consistently propagated the DESI survey ge-
ometry through the window convolution of both statistics. Building on the TripoSH
window formalism of [43], the bispectrum convolution can be implemented as a linear
transformation of the theoretical multipoles, which makes its repeated evaluation in
the likelihood inexpensive. We have tested this procedure on cut-sky mocks and find
that it reproduces the measured bispectrum multipoles over the range of scales used in
the analysis (Figure 10). Our power-spectrum-only constraints are in good agreement
with both the DESI full-shape analysis and the independent BAO+BBN constraints
(Figure 5). The largest differences are found forω cdm in the individual LRG1 and QSO
fits, where the posterior is particularly sensitive to the counterterm priors and to the
nuisance-parameter reparametrisation. Tests on the mean cut-sky mocks nevertheless
recover the fiducial cosmology within the expected uncertainties.
Adding the bispectrum monopole changes the constraints in a way that depends
strongly on the tracer and on the cosmological parameter. The largest and most persis-
tent gains are found forω cdm and ln(1010As), with reductions of up to 18% and 20% in
the marginalised constraints, respectively, in the single-tracer analysis. The improve-
ment varies across the individual redshift bins and becomes different once the samples

translates into stronger constraints when the dark-energy equation of state is allowed
to evolve. In thew 0waCDM model, the extra freedom substantially broadens the cos-
mological posteriors, and the addition ofB 000 produces only a mild change in the
allowedw 0-wa region. The resulting posteriors remain compatible with a cosmological
constant, although the posterior reaches the prior boundaries in the dark-energy pa-
rameter space. Thus, with the present data vector and scale cuts, the main benefit of
the bispectrum is in the determination of the matter-sector and clustering-amplitude
parameters rather than in constraining the time evolution of dark energy.
An important aspect of the analysis is the use of the bispectrum monopole up
tok max = 0.2h/Mpc despite employing a tree-level model. For LRGs, this choice is
supported by the periodic-box tests of [30], where the joint one-loop EFT power spec-
trum and tree-level bispectrum analysis remained unbiased up to approximately this
scale even without phenomenological damping. That work also found that the cross-
correlation betweenP 0 andB 000 becomes large towards smaller scales, suggesting that
the additional bispectrum bins are not providing completely independent information
to the joint likelihood. The cut-sky tests presented here provide the corresponding val-
idation in the presence of the DESI survey geometry and are particularly relevant for
the QSO sample. This empirical scale reach should not be interpreted as a statement
that the perturbative accuracy of the tree-level bispectrum itself extends to arbitrarily
small scales. The present analysis is restricted to diagonal bispectrum configurations
and to the LRG and QSO samples, with the bispectrum model kept at tree level.
Extending the analysis to a larger set of triangle configurations, additional tracers, in
particular ELGs, and higher-order bispectrum modelling will be important for deter-
mining how much more information can be extracted from the higher-order clustering
of future DESI data.

A.2 Convolution test
We validate the bispectrum window convolution using the mean of the 25 altMTL
mocks (see Section 4.1), for all tracers considered in this analysis, as shown in Figure 10.

the mean of the 25 boxes from which the cut-sky mocks were generated, as they come
from the same realisations.
For this test, we considered the GC-combined data vectors and window matrices,
as described in Section 3. These are shown in Figure 10 as the black dots. The error
bars were obtained from the standard deviationσof the 25 measurements. We can
see that both bispectrum multipoles are accurately reproduced through the window
convolution procedure over the whole range of scales we consider in this work (k max =
0.2h/Mpc), as can be seen in the residuals ∆B ℓ1ℓ2L/σ.
For the first two LRG bins, we found the remaining monopole mismatch to be

be used to bypass residuals of the window convolution. The constantP shot parame-

kmax = 0.2h/Mpc.
