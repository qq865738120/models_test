Abstract—While independent and identically distributed noise
is typically assumed in quantum computing research, complex
error models such as correlated and spatio-temporal errors
remain underexplored. In this paper, the performance ofXZZX
and rotated surface codes is evaluated under these complex
noise models. Standard graph-based decoders struggle with the
hyperedges generated by correlated errors on bias-tailored codes.
To address this, we employ the Minimum Weight Parity Factor
(MWPF) decoder, which natively processes syndrome hyper-
graphs without artificial edge decomposition. Our simulations
show that under strong Z-biased circuit-level correlated noise
(η= 100), the threshold of the rotated code degrades to
≈0.36%, while theXZZXcode drops to≈0.70%. Furthermore,
a distinct non-monotonic threshold dependence is observed for
theXZZXcode across different bias ratios. While theXZZXcode
effectively absorbs high-density Z-type hyperedges at extreme
biases, it exhibits a threshold minimum at moderate biases
(η≈10) because transverse X and Y errors actively bridge the
diagonal strings, breaking the code’s one-dimensional decoupling
symmetry. These findings demonstrate that utilizing hypergraph-
aware decoders is critical, as the performance of bias-tailored
codes is highly sensitive to the geometric structure of spatio-
temporal correlated noise.
I. INTRODUCTION
Fault-tolerant quantum computers are essential for realizing
practical quantum computation. Unlike classical computers
that primarily face bit-flip errors, quantum computers expe-
rience a variety of errors due to decoherence, imperfect gate
operations, measurement errors, and environmental noise. To
protect quantum information, several error correction tech-
niques are currently being researched. Surface codes are
currently one of the leading contenders in this race due to
their high error threshold and their ability to operate only
using local nearest-neighbor interactions [1]. To decode errors,
decoders must be highly accurate and capable of solving
complex decoding graphs in real time without introducing
computational bottlenecks.
To reduce the number of physical qubits used to encode
logical states in planar surface codes, rotated surface codes
were developed [1]. By rotating the lattice by 45 ◦, the number
of physical qubits used is reduced by half, while keeping the
code distance and error correction capacity the same. Although
Rotated codes use fewer physical qubits, theXZZXcode [2]
was developed to provide better resistance to biased noise. By
changing the stabilizer generators fromXXXXorZZZZto
XZZX, this code offers more resistance when dealing with bi-
ased noise. Recent work by Kamet al.[3] provides a scalable
framework to model physically motivated, spatio-temporal
correlated noise, demonstrating that such errors cause severe
memory degradation in standard planar surface codes. While
standard codes suffer from generalized threshold degradation,
this paper demonstrates that spatio-temporal correlations in-
troduce unique, geometry-specific vulnerabilities inXZZX
and rotated codes by breaking the fundamental decoupling
symmetries that bias-tailored codes rely on. Furthermore, by
utilizing the Minimum Weight Parity Factor (MWPF) decoder,
we isolate these topological vulnerabilities from the artificial
algorithmic penalties typically introduced by hyper edge de-
composition.
However, the impact of this modeled spatio-temporal noise
on resource-optimized variants, such as rotated andXZZXsur-
face codes, remains unquantified. In practice, on real hardware,
noise is not always independent and identically distributed
(i.i.d). Errors that affect the qubits can also be correlated in
nature [3], [4], [5]. Understanding the exact limits of topo-
logical codes under such complex noise models has become a
major focus in recent literature. Recent studies have analyzed
the precise effect of multiqubit correlated noise on surface
code thresholds [6], while other related work have shown that
certain specific correlation symmetries can counterintuitively
increase the threshold under certain conditions [7]. Exact
statistical-mechanical mappings have also been developed to
map error thresholds strictly under nearest-neighbor correlated
noise [8]. Despite these analytical advantages, a critical gap
remains in the literature. While previous studies have indepen-
dently evaluated standard codes under spatial crosstalk [6],
or bias-tailored codes under purely phenomenological noise
[9], there is no comprehensive evaluation of bias-tailored
geometries under a unified spatio-temporal correlated noise
model. This work bridges that gap by providing a direct
numerical comparison between theXZZXand rotated surface
codes under a dynamic circuit-level detector error model that
introduces both temporal decoherence and spatial crosstalk.
Concurrent with our work, Thakur and Van Huele [10]
studied ZX and ZY surface codes under circuit-level biased
noise with spatial XX crosstalk. They found that MWPM
cannot adequately exploit the doubled syndrome information

In the detector error model framework, a detector corre-
sponds to a parity check over a set of measurement outcomes
that is deterministic in the absence of noise. That is, under
noiseless execution, each detector takes a fixed value across re-
peated runs, resulting in flips of one or more detectors. Unlike
standard noise models—where a single physical error triggers
at most two detectors—correlated errors can trigger three or
more. In graph-based decoders, these multi-detector events
correspond to hyperedges. Because standard decoders assume
noise to be i.i.d, they can process only standard edges, each
connecting exactly two detectors and not hyperedges. This
limitation forces hyperedges to be artificially decomposed.
As a result, the decoder loses critical correlation information
and miscalculates error probabilities, leading to sub-optimal
matchings and a severely degraded threshold. This paper builds
on the framework for quantifying this exact threshold degra-
dation specifically forXZZXand rotated surface codes. By
performing circuit-level simulations in STIM [11] and utilizing
the MWPF decoder, we provide a direct numerical comparison
of these two code families under modeled spatio-temporal
correlated noise. Ultimately, while standard graph decoders
provide an excellent approximation of maximum likelihood
(ML) decoding for standard syndrome graphs, utilizing MWPF
ensures that this near-ML decoding accuracy is preserved
when processing the complex syndrome hypergraphs gener-
ated by modeled hardware noise environments.

II covers the background on surface codes and their variants.
Section III covers the noise models and decoding used, fol-
lowed by Section IV that gives the simulation results. The
paper concludes with a discussion and summary in Section V.

To contextualize the threshold vulnerabilities of bias-
tailored codes under correlated noise, we first review the un-
derlying theoretical frameworks of the surface code geometries
evaluated in this study.

B. Surface Codes
While the stabilizer formalism provides mathematical rules
for error detection, translating them into physical architectures
require scalable geometric layout. A primary constraint in
quantum hardware is the difficulty of engineering long-range
interactions between distant qubits. This also places a con-
straint on the design of any practical quantum error correcting
code, that is logical information must be protected using only
interactions between physically neighboring qubits.
To achieve large code distances and scalable quantum
memory while restricting operations to local nearest-neighbor
interactions, topological codes were introduced. The idea was
first proposed by Kitaev [12], where he showed 2 logical
qubits can be encoded in a two-dimensional lattice shaped as a
toroid with periodic boundary conditions, where the horizontal
row encodes the logical ¯Xand the vertical lines encode the
logical ¯Z. Because implementing periodic boundary conditions
is physically impractical on two-dimensional quantum chip
architectures, planar surface codes with open boundaries were
subsequently developed [1]. Below are some types of surface
codes that are currently being widely used.
1) Rotated Surface Codes:The lattice is rotated by45 ◦
compared to the standard planar construction, allowing a more
efficient use of physical qubits. For a code of distanced, the
rotated surface code [1] requiresn=d 2 data qubits andd 2 −1
ancilla qubits, compared to the(2d−1) 2 data qubits required
by the unrotated planar code. The stabilizers are divided into
two types:X-type andZ-type stabilizers. Each type has d2−1

stabilizers. In this structure data qubits lie on the edges of the
lattice while ancilla qubits are placed at the center of each
face for stabilizer measurements.
2)XZZXSurface Code:TheXZZXsurface code [2] is
a modified variant of the standard surface code which was
designed for improved performance under biased noise. Unlike
conventional surface codes that use purelyXXXXorZZZZ
operators on each plaquettes, theXZZXcode applies mixed

III. NOISEMODELS ANDDECODING
Standard evaluations of surface codes frequently assume
noise to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.).
However, to accurately understand their performance on phys-
ical hardware, it is critical to evaluate their behavior under
correlated, spatio-temporal errors. The primary motivation of
this work lies in quantifying this exact threshold degradation
large-scale simulations across multiple noise biases.
The following subsections detail the noise models and the
decoding algorithms utilized in this work to natively process
and quantify this degradation.
A. Noise
1) Phenomenological noise vs circuit-level noise:In quan-
tum error correction simulations, noise is typically evaluated
at two distinct levels of abstraction: phenomenological and
circuit-level noise. Phenomenological noise models assume
perfect syndrome extraction and errors occurring only on data
qubits and measurement outcomes. On the other hand circuit-
level noise is generally more complex to simulate, they inject
errors directly into the syndrome extraction, state preparation,
entangling gates, idle periods, as well as measurements. It is
simulated in order to capture the physical reality of how noise
can propagate through circuits.
2) Biased Noise:Quantum systems often suffer from asym-
metric error probabilities, called biased noise. An example of
such is a system where pure phase-flip (Z) errors occur at a
much higher rate than bit-flip (X) or combined (Y) errors. To
quantify bias, we must first define biased Pauli noise channel.

B. Spatio-Temporal Errors
While the biased noise model provides a baseline, realistic
systems also undergo spatio-temporal-correlated errors [13],
[14]. To accurately quantify threshold degradation, it is critical
to evaluate the codes under an additive spatio-temporal penalty
framework. If a nominal physical error ratepis simply parti-
tioned into fractional weights across all error mechanisms, the
baseline failure rates for standard operations are suppressed.
Instead, we definepas the true baseline physical error rate
for all standard circuit operations, including entangling gates,
state preparation, and measurements.
Spatio-temporal correlations are then introduced as strictly
additive overheads on top of this baseline. For temporal noise,
continuous memory decoherence is subjected to an overhead
factor off idle = 1.0. For spatial noise, a 50% crosstalk penalty
is distributed equally between adjacent (f adj = 0.25) and
diagonal (f diag = 0.25) correlations. This additive model en-
sures that the performance of the MWPF decoder is measured
against a rigorous correlated noise environment layered over
an uncompromised standard baseline.
To provide a concrete framework for error injection, the
syndrome extraction round is divided into discrete ticks.
Based on the standard circuit architecture generated for these
simulations, a single extraction round consists ofN ticks = 7
discrete clock cycles. Ancilla initialization occurs in the first
tick, where State Preparation and Measurement (SPAM) errors
are injected at the baseline ratep. Entangling gates (CNOT or
CZ) are applied in subsequent ticks, immediately followed by
two-qubit gate errors at the baseline ratep. Finally, ancilla
measurements occur, accompanied by measurement errors at
ratep. During any tick where a qubit is not actively partici-
pating in a gate operation, idle errors are injected to simulate
continuous decoherence. These errors are injected directly into
the syndrome extraction schedule across three distinct physical
mechanisms. They are explained in detail below:
1) Continuous Memory Decoherence:In physical quan-
tum systems exhibiting heavily biased noise, qubits undergo
continuous phase damping (T 2) when idle[cite: 25]. Over
a continuous time intervalt, the probability of a physical
dephasing error grows exponentially as [15]:

2) Faulty Entanglement Gates and Spatial Crosstalk:Two-

physical hook errors and cross-talk that originate from the gate
mechanism and dense physical layouts.
3) Organic Hyperedge F ormation:Rather than artificially
constructing joint probability distributions, correlations emerge
deterministically through Pauli error propagation. For instance,
consider a continuous memoryZfault occurring on a data
qubit mid-schedule. In subsequent ticks, if this data qubit
acts as the control for a CNOT gate targeting anX-measure
ancilla, theZerror commutes with the control and propagates
to the targetX-ancilla, flipping its measurement outcome.
Furthermore, theZerror remains on the data qubit, where it
will flip the subsequent round’sX-ancilla measurements. This
single propagating fault simultaneously corrupts the spatial
data state and flips multiple temporal measurement outcomes.
This organic circuit-level propagation deterministically gen-
erates complex spatio-temporal hyperedges within the detector
error model. A detector error model maps each physical error
mechanismeto the set of detection events whose outcomes
it flips. In the simplest case, an independent error mechanism
flips exactly two detection events (De ={d i, dj}), correspond-
ing to a standard edge. However, under realistic circuit-level
noise, an error may propagate and flip more than two detection
events simultaneously; these multi-detection events correspond
to hyperedges.

scaling of superconducting hardwares [17]. The temporal

roughly equivalent to the baseline two-qubit gate error rate
itself, a common characteristic in architectures whereT 1/T2

observed capacitive crosstalk and microwave pulse leakage to

C. Minimum Weight Parity Factor Decoding
While standard graph-based decoders are highly effective
for independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) noise, they
face fundamental limitations when encountering correlated
spatio-temporal noise. Because traditional pairwise matching
is restricted to a standard graph-based structure, it can only
process edges that connect a maximum of two vertices.
When a spatio-temporal error mechanism triggers a hyper-
edge (three or more detection events), these decoders must
artificially decompose this hyperedge into standard edges. This
decomposition discards the joint probability information of the
correlation, leading to sub-optimal matching and a significant
degradation in the logical error threshold.
To overcome the limitations of edge decomposition in
standard graph based decoders, this work utilizes the MWPF
decoder [21]. Instead of reducing the DEM to a standard graph,
the MWPF operates directly on the hypergraph representation
of the syndrome. For a given set of triggered detectors, the
MWPF algorithm searches for a parity factor—a subgraph of
the hypergraph where the degree of each vertex matches the
observed syndrome parity.
Mathematically it can be represented as a Maximum Like-
lihood (MLE) problem, where the goal is to identify the most
probable error trajectory that is consistent with the observed
syndrome. LetH= (V, E)be the syndrome hypergraph,

indicates that the corresponding error has occurred, andx e = 0

IV. SIMULATION AND RESULTS
A. Simulation Parameters
To evaluate code performance under hyperedges as de-
scribed in Section III, large-scale circuit-level simulations were
conducted. Processing syndrome hypergraphs with the MWPF
decoder isolates true topological resilience, avoiding the arti-
ficial accuracy drops due to edge decomposition. To optimize
runtime, simulations are bound at either 10,000,000 shots or
2,000 errors. This error bound ensures consistent statistical
precision, as relative uncertainty scales as1/ √errors. The core
parameters are:
(a) Code distance (d):{3,5,7,9}
(b) Max Shots:10,000,000
(c) Max Errors:2,000
(d) Round factor: Same as code distance
(e) Data points per curve:20
(f) Physical error rate (p):p∈[0.001, p max], wherep max ∈
[0.050,0.10]. It is adjusted according to the encoder and
noise model to tightly bound their respective threshold
crossings.
To determine the logical error thresholdp th, we identify
the scale-invariant crossing point of the logical error rate
curves for distancesd∈ {3,5,7,9}. The crossing region is
isolated, and the critical point is extracted using a polynomial
interpolation of the data points nearest to the intersection. It
should be noted that all reportedp th values are numerically
estimated thresholds from finite-distance simulations rather
than exact analytical limits. Distancesd∈ {3,5,7,9}were
selected to accurately capture the finite-size crossing behavior
while keeping the computational cost of processing dense
multi-basis hypergraphs manageable.

D. Threshold Dependence on Noise Bias
To fully understand the relationship between spatio-
temporal correlations and code geometry, code perfor-
mance was evaluated under various bias ratios,η∈
{1,10,20,50,100,∞}. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the two codes
respond fundamentally differently to increasing bias under
spatio-temporal noise.
The rotated surface code exhibits a monotonic threshold
degradation, decreasing fromp th ≈0.54%under standard
depolarizing noise (η= 1) top th ≈0.34%under pure
dephasing (η=∞). This predictable decline reflects the
structural symmetry of the code; since it does not natively
targetZ-errors, increasing their proportion uniformly stresses
the topological structure alongside the spatio-temporal hyper-
edges.
In contrast, theXZZXsurface code demonstrates a distinct
non-monotonic dependence. Its threshold initially drops from
pth ≈0.71%atη= 1to a local minimum ofp th ≈0.66%
nearη= 10. However, as the noise becomes overwhelmingly
Z-biased, the threshold recovers, climbing asymptotically to
pth ≈0.70%asη→ ∞.
The non-monotonic threshold dependence observed in the
XZZXcode can be quantitatively understood by comparing the
total hyperedge density against the Pauli error composition.
As demonstrated in Fig. 8, the total density of hyperedges
per syndrome round monotonically increases and asymptotes
withη, driven by the dominant Z-type spatial correlations.
However, at extreme biases (η→ ∞), these dense hyperedges
are almost exclusively Z-type. Because the MWPF decoder
natively processes these hypergraphs without edge decompo-
sition, theXZZXcode successfully absorbs them within its
robust, decoupled 1D repetition chains, allowing the threshold
to recover.
On the other hand, at moderate biases nearη≈10, while the
overall hyperedge density is lower, the noise contains a critical
fraction of non-dominant transverse errors (X and Y). Since
Y=iXZ, a transverse Y-fault simultaneously anticommutes
with both an X-type and a Z-type stabilizer, flipping detectors
of both types in a single event; together with the rarer
independent X-faults, these transverse components actively
bridge the 1D diagonal strings across different bases, effec-
tively coupling the chains and breaking the one-dimensional
decoupling symmetry. This complex, multi-basis hypergraph
topology is highly destructive to theXZZXgeometry, resulting
in the observed threshold minimum before the purely Z-biased
symmetry is restored.
Notably, this behavior is not simply explained by the trans-
verse error fraction alone. The individual transverse error rates
px =p y =p/(η+ 2)decrease monotonically withη, so the

transverse fraction is in factlargestatη= 1rather than at the
observed minimum nearη≈10. The non-monotonic threshold
instead arises from a competition between two quantities
that individually vary withηin opposite directions: the total
hyperedge density, which is low atη= 1and grows with
bias (Fig. 8), and the transverse error fraction, which is high
atη= 1and falls off with bias. Atη= 1, transverse
errors are plentiful but too few hyperedges form overall for
cross-chain bridging to significantly degrade the threshold. At
η→ ∞, hyperedges are dense but almost purely Z-type, so
despite the high density there are too few transverse faults
available to bridge diagonal chains, and the code’s native
resilience to Z-type hyperedges dominates. The intermediate
regime nearη≈10is where both conditions are simultane-
ously satisfied to a damaging degree: hyperedge density has
risen enough to generate frequent multi-detector correlations,
while the transverse fraction remains large enough to seed
cross-chain bridging events. This joint condition, rather than
either quantity in isolation, produces the observed threshold
minimum.
V. DISCUSSION ANDSUMMARY
The central result of this study is that the effect of increas-
ing Z-bias on theXZZXthreshold is non-monotonic under
spatio-temporal correlated noise. Using the MWPF decoder to
natively process correlated syndromes without artificial edge
decomposition, we isolated the true topological response of
the rotated andXZZXsurface codes.
While the rotated code exhibits a predictable, mono-
tonic threshold degradation as noise bias increases, the
XZZXcode’s one-dimensional decoupling symmetry is ac-
tively broken at moderate biases (η≈10). We demon-
strated that it is the mixture of transverse errors with spatio-
temporal correlations—rather than just the absolute volume of
hyperedges—that couples the otherwise independent diagonal
repetition strings. Despite this vulnerability, theXZZXcode
retains a higher simulated threshold than the rotated code
across the modeled regimes.
It is important to note the limitations of this numerical
study. The correlated-noise parameters used (f adj,f diag,f idle)
represent specific model assumptions designed to stress the
topological structures, rather than direct characterizations of
a specific physical hardware platform. Furthermore, while
the MWPF decoder effectively bounded the asymptotic code
behavior, larger code distances and experimental validation
on physical superconducting architectures remain necessary to
confirm these thresholds.
Future work should explore bias-preserving gate designs and
noise-aware decoding strategies to better maintain theXZZX
advantage under physical hardware conditions, especially as
systems scale below surface code thresholds [22], [23].
