From Syndromes to Computation: A General Framework for Logical Operations in
Quantum Error-Correcting Codes
Hunter Nelson,1, 2,∗ Arshag Danageozian,1, 2 ChunJun Cao,1, 2 Sophia E. Economou,1, 2 and Edwin Barnes1, 2,†
1Department of Physics, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, VA 24061, USA
2Virginia Tech Center for Quantum Information Science and Engineering, Blacksburg, VA 24061, USA
(Dated: October 14, 2026)
Fault-tolerant quantum computation requires error-correcting codes that not only protect quan-
tum information but also support useful logical operations with practical physical realizations. Here
we develop a general framework in which the stabilizer syndromes of commuting Pauli rotations
directly organize logical-gate design. We derive an exact codespace-preservation condition valid
at arbitrary rotation angles, providing a common stabilizer-level description for CSS and non-CSS
codes and for both Clifford and non-Clifford operations. A particularly useful equal-syndrome spe-
cialization reduces Clifford gate design to stabilizer factorization, yields a general generator weight
bound of⌈d/2⌉for distance-dcodes, and systematically extends to logical rotations higher in the
Clifford hierarchy. Applied to bivariate-bicycle (BB) and non-CSS Mirror qLDPC codes, the frame-
work gives explicit low-weight Clifford and controlled-phase gates. Our implementations yield new
low-weight gates, including an addressedTgate on the[[16,4,4]]BB code,T-type rotations on the
[[64,8,8]]BB code, and CCZ-, CS-, andT-type operations on the[[36,6,6]]Mirror code. We also
develop analytic constructions using Clifford algebra bases, which turn the syndrome constraints
into explicit algebraic relations and provide a systematic route to logical addressability. We use
this approach to analytically construct addressed CNOT, SWAP, controlled, and CCZ-type gates
on recursive hypercube codes. An independent construction on middle quantum Reed–Muller codes
yields CrZ gates that saturate the equal-syndrome weight bound. These results establish stabilizer
syndromes as a constructive link between the stabilizer structure of a quantum code, the logical
computations it can directly support, and the fault-tolerance properties of their physical realiza-
tions.
I. INTRODUCTION
Quantum error correction is essential to scalable quan-
tum computation, both for protecting quantum infor-
mation against noise and for enabling long-depth logi-
cal processing [1–5]. Over the past three decades, the
field has developed increasingly sophisticated code fam-
ilies with improved trade-offs among distance, rate, lo-
cality, and overhead, including concatenated, topolog-
ical, color-code, and quantum low-density-parity-check
(qLDPC) constructions [6–15]. For such codes to form
useful computational architectures, however, error pro-
tection is only one part of the overall design. One must
also understand how to perform a sufficiently rich set of
logical operations while preserving the advantages of the
encoding [5, 16, 17]. The design of logical gates should
therefore be intertwined with the design of the codes
themselves.
A number of complementary paradigms have been de-
veloped for implementing logical gates fault-tolerantly.
Transversal gates are especially attractive because their
restricted support strongly limits the propagation of er-
rors within a code block [18, 19]. Their computational
power, however, is fundamentally limited by the Eastin–
Knill theorem, which rules out universal transversal gate
sets [20], while locality-preserving logical gates inD-

these developments make it natural to seek structured
logical-gate constructions beyond strict transversality.

challenge. Given a stabilizer code and a target logical

A second challenge arises beyond the Clifford group.
Clifford gates are relatively more amenable to systematic
analysis because conjugation by a Clifford unitary maps
Pauli operators into Pauli operators. Code preservation
and logical action can therefore be expressed directly
in the binary symplectic language of the stabilizer for-
malism, enabling general logical-Clifford synthesis, auto-
morphism searches, and structural classifications of code-
preserving Clifford circuits [24, 41–44]. For non-Clifford
gates this closure is lost, and the same stabilizer ma-
chinery no longer directly provides a general codespace-
preservation criterion. Systematic results are known for
restricted physical ansätze, including transversalTgates
and more general transversal diagonal Clifford-hierarchy
operations [45–47]. Recent constructions for qLDPC
and related high-rate quantum codes have gone sub-
stantially further, realizing transversal or constant-depth
non-Clifford gates through intersection forms, multipli-
cation properties, cup products, higher symmetries, and
special sheaf, product, or manifold structure [36, 38–
40, 48–54]. These results demonstrate that non-Clifford
computation is compatible with high-rate and qLDPC
codes, but the known constructions are typically tied to
additional algebraic or geometric structure beyond the
stabilizercommutationrelationsthemselves. Theseprop-
erties are not guaranteed for an arbitrary stabilizer code
and must first be established before the corresponding
gate construction can be applied.
Recent work has begun to narrow this gap. Ref. [55]
developsasystematicapproachtoweaklytransversallog-
ical gates in general CSS codes, including a procedure for
constructing physical Pauli rotations whose logical action
is realized probabilistically through syndrome measure-
ment and conditional feedforward. The very recently in-
troduced chain-map hierarchy provides a complementary
homological framework for systematically constructing
Pauli, Clifford, and non-Clifford logical operations, in-
cluding constant-depth Clifford circuits and non-Clifford
controlled-phase gates [37]. Additionally, another very
recent contribution constructs CSS codes supporting uni-
versal logical gate sets by coupling lower-distance seed
codes admitting non-Clifford gates to auxiliary kernel
codes through entangling circuits. The resulting logi-
cal gates are implemented by depth-one layers of disjoint
few-qubit gates, potentially using different physical-qubit
partitions [56].
However, these approaches never answer a more di-
rect question in the stabilizer language itself: Given only
the stabilizers of a code, how can we systematically con-
struct unitary circuits that preserve the codespace and
implement nontrivial logical gates? Such a general ap-
proach would apply to both CSS and non-CSS codes,
handleCliffordandnon-Cliffordrotationsunderthesame
preservation condition, and avoid requiring syndrome
measurement, or an additional chain-complex or geomet-
ricconstruction. Itwouldalsoofferadirectstartingpoint
for systematic gate searches on existing codes and ana-
lytic constructions across multiple levels of the Clifford
hierarchy.
In this work, we develop such a drastically simplify-
ing general framework in which stabilizer syndromes be-
come an active design principle for logical gate design.
We consider logical gate ansätze (LGAs) built from mu-
tually commuting Pauli rotations and derive an exact
codespace-preservation condition valid at arbitrary ro-
tation angles. The stabilizer syndrome of each generator
records which stabilizer checks select (i.e., are altered by)
that rotation under conjugation, so the syndrome pattern
identifies the doubled-angle operation that each stabilizer
check selects. Codespace preservation requires every such
operation to act as the identity on the codespace. Group-
ing the generators by these patterns gives what we call a
syndrome partition. Because the condition is formulated
directly in the stabilizer and Pauli-operator language, it
applies uniformly to CSS and non-CSS codes and at both
Clifford and non-Clifford rotation angles. It therefore
provides a common stabilizer-level starting point for con-
structing logical gates beyond both strict transversality
and the Clifford setting.
A useful realization of this principle occurs when all
Pauli generators share the same nonzero syndrome. In
this setting, pairwise products of the Pauli generators
have zero syndrome, so the LGA can be organized di-
rectly by its relative logical Pauli content. At the Clifford
angleπ/4, gate design reduces to factoring a stabilizer
into commuting equal-syndrome Pauli generators and
choosing physical representatives with favorable weight
and support. We prove that any nontrivial construction
of this form on a distance-dcode must contain a Pauli
generator of weight at least⌈d/2⌉. The same viewpoint
also organizes and simplifies the construction of gates
higher in the Clifford hierarchy. In particular, equal-
syndrome pairs provide a systematic way to construct ro-
tations about a chosen logical Pauli axis, yielding logical
π/4phase rotations andπ/8T-type rotations. More gen-
erally, syndrome relations between lower-level gates re-
veal how they can be combined to obtain higher-level op-
erations. Thus the same stabilizer-syndrome framework
connects Clifford and non-Clifford gate design within a
single construction principle.

including self-dual bivariate-bicycle codes and the non-
CSS Mirror code. The resulting searches produce large
catalogs of logical Clifford operations together with sev-
eral direct non-Clifford constructions. Our low-weight
implementations include an addressedTgate on the

A second major contribution is an analytic route to
constructing solutions of the syndrome-partition condi-
tions. We introduce Clifford algebra bases (CABs) as
a coordinate system in which stabilizers, logical Paulis,
and Pauli operators carrying nonzero syndrome share a
common index structure. Pauli multiplication and sta-
bilizer syndromes then reduce to simple relations among
these indices, making equal-syndrome constructions ac-
cessible analytically. We demonstrate this on recursive
hypercube codes, where simple CAB-index patterns sys-
tematically produce addressed logical CNOT and SWAP
gates, more general controlled operations, and higher-
level gates such as CCZ, all with structured physical re-
alizations. In a separate syndrome based analytic con-
struction, we show that middle quantum Reed–Muller
codes can support logical CrZ gates whose generators
have disjoint support and weight exactlyd/2, saturating
the general equal-syndrome bound.
The low-weight and low-depth Pauli-rotation realiza-
tions found throughout this work are also favorable from
a fault-tolerance perspective. Many of the gates use few-
qubit Pauli generators arranged into one or a small num-
ber of Pauli-rotation layers with disjoint support within
each layer. Small generator supports limit the initial
spread of an error, while low Pauli-rotation depth pro-
vides natural points at which intermediate error cor-
rection can be inserted when successive layers overlap.
Beyond these geometric properties, the syndrome infor-
mation used to construct the gates also contains fault-
tolerance information. Relations among the generator
syndromes can identify small collections of Pauli-rotation
supports that can carry logical operators, while the rank
of the generator-syndrome span determines how many
independent stabilizer combinations remain unchanged
throughout the gate.
Together, these results establish syndrome partitions
as a general design principle for direct logical gates. This
places CSS and non-CSS codes, as well as Clifford and
non-Clifford gates, within the same construction frame-
work. Importantly, the same syndrome structure used to
construct a logical gate also constrains its fault-tolerance
properties, including how many Pauli-rotation supports
can combine to carry a logical operator and which stabi-
lizer combinations remain available during the gate. Syn-
drome partitions therefore provide a systematic route not
only to uncovering direct logical operations in existing
codes, but also to searching for implementations with fa-
vorable fault-propagation structure and to designing new
codes together with their logical gate sets. More broadly,
thissuggestsaroutetowardco-designingerrorcorrection,
logical computation, and fault-tolerant implementation
within a common stabilizer-syndrome framework.

II. LOGICAL-GATE DESIGN FROM
SYNDROME PARTITIONS
Logicalgatesin stabilizercodeshavebeenstudied from
many different perspectives. Transversal constructions
require the logical gate to factor into physical opera-
tions that act independently across corresponding physi-
cal qubits of different code blocks, so that a single phys-
ical fault cannot spread within a code block. One then
asks which logical operations are compatible with this re-
stricted form [18–20, 57, 58]. Code-automorphism meth-
ods exploit physical symmetries, such as qubit permu-
tations or local Clifford transformations, that preserve
the stabilizer group. The corresponding action on the
logical Pauli operators then determines the induced log-
ical Clifford gate [23, 24]. Other algebraic and homolog-
ical approaches represent the code through symplectic,
chain-complex, cohomological, or related structures, and
construct logical gates as transformations that preserve
those structures while acting nontrivially on the encoded
degrees of freedom [37, 42]. In this work, instead of rely-
ingonapredeterminedtransversalpattern, aknowncode
symmetry, or a specific homological construction, we fo-
cus on commuting physical Pauli rotations as our funda-
mental building blocks of logical gates. The main chal-
lenge is to then find sets of Pauli generators whose sta-
bilizer syndromes are compatible with codespace preser-
vation.
Ourgoalistogobeyondstrictlytransversalgateswhile
retaining physical structure that is favorable for fault tol-
erance. In particular, we allow the physical Pauli rota-
tions to act on several qubits within the same code block,
which considerably enlarges the space of possible logi-
cal gates. At the same time, we seek implementations
built from low-weight rotations that, where possible, act
on disjoint sets of physical qubits or can be divided into
only a small number of layers. Disjointness limits how far
an error can spread during a single layer, while a small

not by themselves guarantee a fault-tolerant implementa-

EXISTING QLDPC CODES
The constructions of the previous section turn
codespace preservation into algebraic conditions on the
syndromes and relative logical actions of the physical
Pauli generators making up Eq. (6). We now use those
conditions as search rules for direct logical gates on ex-
isting qLDPC codes. In addition to finding nontrivial
logical operations, we look for physically simple realiza-
tions with low Pauli generator weight and low Pauli-
rotation depth, so that the resulting Pauli rotations act

on small and, where possible, disjoint sets of physical
qubits. The accompanying JSON file contains the signed

plete Pauli-rotation generators for the code-preserving
LGAs found in this work.
To begin, we use the syndrome framework to construct
catalogs of logical Clifford gates on existing qLDPC
codes. First, we factor stabilizers into disjoint, low-
weight Pauli generators with a common nonzero syn-
drome, yielding code-preserving Clifford LGAs. Second,
we combine equal-syndrome pairs whose products rep-
resent the same logical PauliLso that three suitably
related pairs realize a logical rootRL(π/4). These two
constructions generate complementary catalogs of logical
Clifford gates. We then use these Clifford gates to con-
struct gates one level higher in the Clifford hierarchy us-
ing the methods in Secs. IIE and IIF. We also search di-
rectly for non-Clifford gates in both settings. Rank-three
common-syndrome cubes yield CCZ-type gates, while
complete seven-pair structures yield logical Pauli roots
at angleπ/8.
We apply these searches primarily to the self-dual
bivariate-bicycle (BB) codes [68], whose previously stud-
ied logical-gate mechanisms are predominantly Clifford
[24, 42, 68, 69]. This makes them a useful setting for
demonstrating how the syndrome constructions above
can produce additional low-weight Clifford gates and op-

the non-CSS[[36,6,6]]Mirror code of Ref. [70] to test
the applicability of our constructions beyond the CSS

A. Clifford gates from direct stabilizer search
We begin with the equal-syndrome Clifford construc-
tion of Sec. IIC. In the qLDPC searches below we restrict
attention to four-generator LGAs with Pauli-rotation
depth one. At physical angleπ/4, such an LGA preserves
the code when thefour commutingPauli generatorsshare
a common nonzero syndrome and, with the appropriate
rotation signs, their product is a+1stabilizer.
We restrict the direct Clifford searches to four Pauli
generators because this is the smallest nontrivial equal-
syndrome construction at angleπ/4. If allqgenerators
have the same nonzero syndromev, then their product
has syndromeqvmodulo two. Since theπ/4preserva-

syndrome must vanish, soqmust be even. Forq= 2,
the productG 1G2 is itself a stabilizer, so the two gen-

has no nontrivial logical action. The first nontrivial case
is thereforeq= 4.
This approach provides a direct way to search for
codespace-preserving Clifford LGAs. For the BB codes,
we begin with a stabilizer and ask if it can be split into
four low-weight, pairwise-disjoint Pauli generators with
the same nonzero syndrome. A proposed factorization
is retained only after the equal-syndrome and stabilizer-
product conditions are verified and the rotation signs
are chosen so that the specified codespace is preserved.
We then compute the induced action in a fixed logical
Pauli frame. Alternatively, one can first enumerate low-
weight Pauli generators, group them by their stabilizer
syndrome, and look for four mutually disjoint Pauli gen-
erators whose product is a stabilizer. This is the strat-
egy used for part of the Mirror search. Throughout the
searches in this work, a weight cutoffwmax on Pauli rota-
tion generators is applied independentlywt(Gj)≤w max,
so the search includes all mixed Pauli-generator weight
profiles within this bound and does not require the gen-
erators in an LGA to have equal weight.
The finite search domain depends on the code size.
For the[[16,4,4]]BB code, we enumerate the full stabi-
lizer group and search for four-generator disjoint factor-
izations with Pauli generator weight at most four. For
the larger code instances, the BB and Mirror construc-
tions are defined on periodic lattices with translationally
related stabilizer checks which we can use to organize the
search space. For the[[64,8,8]]BB code, we search prod-
ucts of at most three translated stabilizer checks, with
stabilizer weight at most24and Pauli generator weight
at most six. For the Mirror code, we use translation
symmetry to identify translation-related Pauli generators
and search one representative from each symmetry orbit,
both at weight four and in a mixed-weight search through
weight six. Full search details are given in Appendix C.

IV. ANALYTIC SYNDROME-PARTITION
CONSTRUCTIONS
The direct stabilizer searches of Sec. IIIA choose phys-
ical Pauli generators from syndrome, commutation, and
support constraints without prescribing a target logical
gate. A logical Pauli frame is used afterward to deter-
mine the action of each accepted LGA. For analytic gate
design, we instead choose the logical frame and desired
gate structure upfront and use aClifford algebra basis
(CAB) presentation to relate them directly to physical
Pauli operators. This makes logical addressability an in-
put to the construction.
A CAB is a convenient generating set for the Pauli
group whose products can be labeled by sets of CAB in-
dices. Multiplication, commutation, and stabilizer syn-
drome can then be computed from simple operations on
these index sets. This allows stabilizers, logical Pauli
operators, and syndrome-carrying physical operators to
be characterized within the same coordinate system. We
first relate CAB coordinates to the usual binary symplec-
tic Pauli representation and then use them to construct
Clifford and higher-level logical gates. Figure 3 summa-
rizes this organization.

D. Recursive CAB syndrome partitions on
hypercube codes
We next apply the CAB construction to hypercube
codes, a family built recursively by concatenating small
error-detecting codes [17]. CABs are particularly useful
for these codes because their recursive structure allows
the same CAB organization to reappear at successive en-
coded levels. Related many-hypercube and subsystem
constructions have also been developed to improve code
parameters and reduce syndrome-measurement overhead
[72,73]. Thesmallerror-detectingcodesunderlyingthese
constructions, as well as the first error-correcting hyper-
cube level, are also directly relevant to recent experimen-
tal logical-qubit architectures [74].
Here we use the hierarchy generated from the[[4,2,2]]
seed code to show how recursive CAB structure can be
turned into logical gates. The recursion can be viewed
as concatenation. At each level, we take four copies of
the previous hypercube code as the inner code and, for
each inner-code logical qubit, encode its four copies us-
ing an outer[[4,2,2]]code. The CAB construction is
especially useful because the same quartic CAB-index
sets and gate templates can then be reused at each con-
catenation level. Starting from simple equal-syndrome
CNOT and SWAP constructions on the[[4,2,2]]code, we
construct addressed Clifford gates at higher levels, low-
weight gates obtained by recursively promoting lower-
level gates, and analytic non-Clifford constructions for
CCZ-type gates.

A. Depth-one fault propagation and Pauli-rotation
faults
Many constructions above have Pauli-rotation depth
one, so all of their Pauli rotations have pairwise-disjoint
physical support. Although these LGAs are not transver-
sal in the usual sense, their disjoint supports restrict fault
propagation because a single-qubit fault can overlap at

Pauli error can overlap at mostwactive Pauli generators.
We therefore track how physical Pauli errors propagate
through each depth-one LGA, treating every multiqubit
Pauli rotation as an ideal operation.

a Pauli of weight eight while the resulting error remains
correctable. More generally, this behavior is expected as
a logical gate preserves the correctability of an error set
according to the Knill–Laflamme conditions, regardless
of the physical weights of the propagated errors.
For Clifford LGAs, the propagated errors are again
Pauli operators, so this correctability is directly visible
from the stabilizer syndromes. For non-Clifford LGAs,
a propagated Pauli error can instead become a coherent
sum of Pauli components. The full conjugated error re-

individual Pauli components of the non-Clifford trans-
formed Pauli errors share a stabilizer syndrome while be-
longing to different stabilizer cosets. Therefore, ordinary
stabilizer syndrome extraction will not always work for
these non-Clifford propagated errors.

B. Intermediate correction for higher-depth gates
Further considerations are needed for gates with Pauli-
rotation depth greater than one. Rotations within one
Pauli-rotation layer have disjoint support, but a data
qubit may participate in another rotation in a later layer.
An error that remains detectable after one layer can
therefore spread further when subsequent overlapping ro-
tations are applied.
A natural approach is pieceable fault tolerance, in
which a nontransversal logical gate is divided into pieces
separated by intermediate error correction [82]. The
support-disjoint Pauli-rotation layers found here provide
natural candidate pieces. This does not replace the re-
quirement that each physical Pauli rotation itself be im-
plemented fault-tolerantly; rather, the intermediate cor-
rection prevents faults from continuing to spread through
later overlapping layers. However, care is needed because
these intermediate layers need not preserve the codespace
and therefore may modify the stabilizers available for in-
termediate error correction.

VI. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
The syndrome-partition framework is formulated at
the level of commuting multiqubit Pauli rotations. This
level of description is useful because it exposes the stabi-
lizer structure of the logical gate without committing to
a particular hardware decomposition, but it also sets the
scope of the present results. In particular, disjoint physi-
cal supports do not by themselves imply a shallow circuit
onceeachmultiqubitrotationiscompiledintoelementary
operations. The resulting circuit depth, connectivity re-
quirements, and opportunities for parallel execution will
depend on the native interactions of the underlying plat-
form. Developing architecture-aware decompositions of
the constructions reported here is therefore an important
next step.
The same distinction applies to the fault analysis.
Treating each multiqubit Pauli rotation as an elementary
operation, we find that the single-fault errors considered
remain distinguishable by their stabilizer syndromes af-
ter propagation through the LGA and are therefore cor-
rectable within this model. This indicates that the sep-
arate support structure of many constructions can of-
fer advantageous fault-propagation properties. It does
not, however, constitute a full circuit-level fault-tolerance
proof. Decomposing a multiqubit rotation into elemen-
tary gates can introduce additional fault locations and
correlated propagation that are not captured here. Es-
tablishing fault-tolerant implementations on specific ar-

[PDF page 28]
28
chitectures will require analyzing these decompositions
together with syndrome extraction and recovery.
The computational results should likewise be inter-
preted within their stated search domains. The qLDPC
searches are exhaustive over the specified Pauli gen-
erator weights, Pauli sectors, syndrome relations, and
stabilizer-equivalent representatives, but they do not es-
tablish global optimality over all possible physical real-
izations. Broader Pauli ansätze, different syndrome em-
beddings, and alternative stabilizer dressings may pro-
duce still simpler realizations. More generally, the frame-
work separates the algebraic question of whether a code-
preserving logical gate exists from the subsequent opti-
mization of its physical implementation.
One conceptual feature of the framework is that sev-
eral apparently different routes to logical gates at higher
levels of the Clifford hierarchy can be formulated us-
ing the same syndrome data. Equal-syndrome factoriza-
tions directly expose logical structure, equal-syndrome
pairs provide systematic rotations about chosen logical
Pauli axes, and relations among lower-level gates can be
used to construct operations higher in the Clifford hier-
archy. These mechanisms are algebraically distinct, but
the same codespace-preservation condition governs them
all. This is particularly useful beyond the Clifford group,
where ordinary symplectic stabilizer methods no longer
provide a complete description of the conjugated checks.
Fault-tolerance considerations also suggest a natural
extension of the syndrome-partition approach. Many of
the constructions studied here rely on pairwise syndrome
relations, but more general relations involving larger col-
lections of Pauli rotations could be used to design gates
with greater tolerance to faults in the physical rotations.
This suggests incorporating fault-tolerance criteria di-
rectly into future syndrome-partition searches, alongside
the generator weight and Pauli-rotation depth already
considered here. The many physically distinct realiza-
tions of the same logical operation found by our searches
provide additional freedom for such optimization, includ-
ing the choice of how errors can spread and which stabi-
lizer checks remain available between successive layers.
Several extensions are therefore natural. The search
methods can be applied to larger qLDPC families and to
higher levels of the Clifford hierarchy, while a broader
theoretical question is to identify which features of a
stabilizer code predict the existence of low-weight, low-
overlap syndrome partitions. Extending the construction
principlesbeyondLGAswouldfurtherenlargetheclassof
accessible physical ansätze. On the analytic side, Clifford
algebracoordinatesmayrevealadditionalrecursivecode–
gate constructions, while the independent Reed–Muller
results suggest that useful syndrome-partition structure
may also be identifiable directly in other algebraic code
families. These directions point toward a more system-
atic form of code–gate co-design in which error-correction
properties and logical operations are optimized together.
Machine learning and AI-assisted search methods
could help explore logical gate ansätze beyond the equal-
syndrome constructions considered in this work. Once
an LGA has been specified, we can verify the codespace-
preservation conditions exactly. However, the more chal-
lenging task involves identifying effective collections of
Pauli generators and syndrome relations. AI-based ap-
proaches could help propose more general syndrome
partitions and optimize the constructions for physical
weight, Pauli-rotation depth, and other fault-tolerance
criteria.
The search space can also expand by relaxing the
commuting-generator assumption in our LGA construc-
tions and integrating syndrome-based searches with code
automorphisms. This would enable the joint exploration
of Pauli-rotation layers, permutations, and logical ac-
tions. An alternative direction is to invert the design
process considered here by starting with the logical gates
and using them to impose stabilizer relations. These
gates could then be completed into codes with the desired
distance and other relevant properties. The syndrome-
partition framework can also be integrated with mod-
ular code-construction approaches, such as Quantum
LEGO [83] and its Power-up framework [35]. Such inves-
tigations might include useful syndrome-partition struc-
tures on small code blocks and determining the compati-
bility or symmetry conditions that ensure the persistence
of corresponding logical operations when these blocks are
assembled into larger codes. We leave such explorations
and AI-assisted generalizations of and improvements to
our framework for future research.
In summary, we have introduced syndrome partitions
as a general design principle for direct logical gates built
from commuting Pauli rotations. The central result is
an exact codespace-preservation condition, valid at arbi-
trary rotation angles, that depends directly on the sta-
bilizer syndromes of the Pauli generators. The condition
applies uniformly to CSS and non-CSS stabilizer codes
and at both Clifford and non-Clifford angles. Its equal-
syndrome specialization turns Clifford gate design into
a stabilizer-factorization problem, bounds the minimum
generator weight in terms of the code distance, and orga-
nizes systematic constructions of logical Pauli rotations
and gates higher in the Clifford hierarchy.
We demonstrated the framework in complementary
computational and analytic settings. Searches on CSS
bivariate-bicycle and non-CSS Mirror qLDPC codes un-
covered large families of Clifford operations together
with direct controlled-phase and logicalT-type rota-
tions. Clifford algebra bases provide an analytic route
to syndrome-partition solutions, producing addressed
CNOT, SWAP, controlled, and CCZ-type operations on
recursive hypercube codes. Independently, middle quan-
tum Reed–Muller codes admit disjoint controlled-Zcon-
structions whose physical weights saturate the general
equal-syndrome bound.
The same syndrome information also exposes struc-
ture relevant to fault tolerance. Relations among the
logical gate generator syndromes constrain how small a
collection of Pauli-rotation supports can carry a logical

[PDF page 29]
29
operator, while the independent syndrome directions de-
termine which stabilizer combinations remain unchanged
throughout a gate and may therefore be available for in-
termediate error correction.
Together, theseresultsshowthatthestabilizerdescrip-
tion of a quantum code can serve as a common framework
for error correction, logical-gate construction, and fault-
tolerance-aware design. Syndrome partitions therefore
provide a route toward co-designing the error-correcting,
computational, and fault-tolerance properties of encoded
quantum systems.

Appendix B: Clifford syndrome-partition search
This appendix describes the general computational
procedure used to identify code-preserving Clifford syn-
drome partitions. The same machinery underlies the
direct qLDPC searches and, with the CAB-guided re-
strictions introduced later, the hypercube searches. The
combinatorialstageworksinthebinaryPauligroupmod-
ulo scalar phase. Each accepted support pattern is then
lifted to Hermitian Pauli representatives, and a linear
system overF 2 determines rotation signs that preserve
the specified+1stabilizer sector. We first formulate the
binary stabilizer factorization problem, then describe the
recursive exact-cover enumeration and the rotation-sign
determination.
1. Stabilizer-factorization search
A convenient way to organize an equal-syndrome Clif-
ford search is to begin with a stabilizer multiplierRrep-
resented in the binary Pauli group modulo scalar phase
and a desired numberqof Pauli generators. We seek a
partition of its nonidentity support,
supp(R) =Q 1 ⊔Q 2 ⊔ · · · ⊔Q q,(B1)
and define the binary Pauli generatorGj =R| Qj by re-
stricting the Pauli letter pattern ofRto the qubits in
Qj. Because the supports are disjoint, the Pauli gener-
ators commute automatically and their phase-free prod-
uct isR. The remaining combinatorial condition is that
the Pauli generators satisfy the chosen weight restrictions
and carry the same nonzero stabilizer syndrome,
σS(G1) =· · ·=σ S(Gq)̸=0.(B2)
At the Clifford angle, such a factorization is a binary can-
didate for a code-preserving LGA. The Hermitian phases
of the Pauli generators and the rotation directions are
fixed in the later signed codespace verification step so
that every selected stabilizer check returns specifically to
the+1sector.

This multiplier-first organization is particularly effec-

when a manageable set of candidate multipliers can be
enumerated. The complementary Pauli generator-first
organization used in some of the direct qLDPC searches
begins instead with physical Pauli generators, groups
them by syndrome, and tests whether suitable products
lie in the stabilizer group. The two procedures impose
the same algebraic preservation condition but explore the
physical search space in different ways.
2. Admissible Pauli generators and exact-cover
enumeration
For a fixed multiplierR, we first enumerate the ad-
missible Pauli generators. A candidate Pauli generator
is specified by a subsetQ⊆supp(R)and inherits from
Rthe physical Pauli acting on every qubit inQ. Candi-
dates are discarded if they violate the specified weight or
any additional support restriction. Their full stabilizer
syndromes are then computed and the candidates are
grouped by nonzero syndrome. The exact-cover recur-
sion can be applied independently within each syndrome
class; when an analytic construction supplies a target
syndrome, the search may instead be restricted to that
class from the outset.
The remaining problem is an exact cover: choose ex-
actlyqadmissible Pauli generators whose supports are
mutually disjoint and whose union issupp(R). We solve
this problem by recursive backtracking. At each stage,
the algorithm chooses an uncovered physical qubit and
branches over the surviving admissible Pauli generators
containing that qubit. Once one Pauli generator is se-
lected, all candidates overlapping it are removed from
that branch.
The implementation uses a deterministic recursive
cover. At each stage it chooses the lowest-index uncov-
ered qubit and branches over admissible Pauli generators
that contain that qubit and are contained in the remain-
ing uncovered support. A branch is terminated when the
remainingsupportcontainsfewerqubitsthanthenumber
of Pauli generators still required. In the final slot, the en-
tire uncovered mask must itself be one of the admissible
Pauli generators. A branch is accepted only when ev-
ery nonidentity position ofRhas therefore been covered
exactly once using the prescribed number of Pauli gen-
erators. This recursion enumerates the unordered covers
in the stated finite Pauli generator domain; the current
implementation does not rely on a minimum-remaining-
values heuristic.
