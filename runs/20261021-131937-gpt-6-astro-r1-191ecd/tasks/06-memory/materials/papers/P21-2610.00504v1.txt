1Department of Computing, Imperial College London, 180 Queen ’s Gate, SW7 2AZ, UK

Post-selection improves the logical reliability of quantum computation by rejecting shots which
are likely to result in logical failure. We introduce graph neural networks that predict decoder failure
without additional decoder executions, using only syndromes, and for high-rate quantum low-density
parity-check (qLDPC) codes, existing belief-propagation posteriors. We evaluate rotated surface and
bivariate bicycle (BB) code memories under uniform depolarising circuit-level noise. Retaining 90%
of shots gives approximately 3700×and 740×reductions in logical error rates for [[72,12,6]] and
[[144,12,12]] BB codes at realistic physical error rates. For the distance 5 surface code at physical
noisep= 0.003, our neural post-selector achieves logical error suppression comparable to the leading
complementary gap method without running the decoder.
I. INTRODUCTION
Current quantum computers have hundreds of noisy
qubits, and, at present, do not achieve quantum advan-
tage over conventional means for operationally useful ap-
plications. Quantum error correction (QEC) is neces-
sary to enable large-scale fault-tolerant quantum compu-
tation, and improving the efficiency of QEC protocols is
central to efforts to build industrially- and scientifically-
relevant quantum computers. Recent years have seen
rapid progress on both the theoretical side of QEC – such
as the introduction of novel and more efficient quantum
codes [1–3] and decoders [4–6] – and on the experimental
side, where small scale experimental demonstrations of
codes and logical operations have been achieved [7–9].
An important technique used to achieve low logical er-
ror rates (LER) in experiments ispost-selection: iden-
tifying which shots are most likely to result in a logical
error – instances in which the decoder fails – and reject-
ing them. While the LER decreases exponentially with
code distance below the threshold, the low error rates
necessary for practical applications require large code dis-
tances and, consequently, a substantial qubit overhead.
Post-selection on the other hand allows one to achieve
LER comparable to those of larger codes with a fraction
of the qubits and is thus expected to be a critical tool to
enable useful fault-tolerant quantum computing in the
near future [10]. The price to pay for this reduction in
qubits is a loweryield(the fraction of shots accepted),
which increases runtime.
The trade-off between LER and yield is favourable
whenever discarding a shot is cheap relative to incur-
ring a logical fault, for instance in magic state prepa-
ration [13] and distillation [10], scalable preparation of
resource states [14], and logical error mitigation, where
decoder or syndrome information is used to either reject
shots [15, 16], or to condition the mitigation [17].

Our work treats post-selection as a binary classifica-
tion task: each shot is labelled withY= 1 if the de-
coder succeeds andY= 0 if it fails. The architecture
is decoder-agnostic, with the caveat that qLDPC codes
require a BP-based decoder. Labelled data can be gen-
erated cheaply and on demand by simulation, as we do
here, or directly by hardware, for example by a quantum
memory experiment that checks whether the decoder pre-
serves a known logical state.
Our contributions are:
•A learned graph neural post-selector for
qLDPC codes.We evaluate the FMPNN on the
[[72,12,6]] and [[144,12,12]] bivariate bicycle (BB)
codes [3] under circuit-level noise. The FMPNN
achieves the best LER suppression (Table I) vs
yield trade-offs at all rejection rates amongst meth-
ods that do not execute the decoder multiple times.
It further matches or outperforms the three-round
logical-error criterion (3R-LEC) variant of argu-
ment reweighting [12], which requires 3 passes of
the decoder.
•A specialised model for surface codes.The
MPNN requires only the syndrome at inference,
meaning it can be executed before decoding. We
evaluate it on the rotated surface codes at dis-
tances 5,7,9 under circuit-level noise. The MPNN
achieves the best LER suppression at all rejec-
tion rates amongst methods which do not execute
the decoder multiple times. At distance 5 and
p= 3×10 −3, it achieves similar LER suppres-
sion at all rejection rates to the complementary
gap [18], the leading post-selection strategy for sur-
face codes, with higher throughput.
•A cost analysis.We give the graph-size de-
pendence of the neural post-selection models (Sec-
tion III F) and measure the per-shot processing
times empirically, comparing against other common
baselines on the same hardware.
Recently, several works have proposed post-selection
criteria for QEC. These approaches fall into three broad
families.
The first reuses decoder confidence: posterior es-
timates, BP log-likelihood ratios (LLRs), or cluster-
level soft information from localized statistics decoding
(LSD) [11, 15, 16, 19, 20]. These criteria can be inexpen-
sive to extract, but depend on the decoder used.
The second family compares repeated decoder execu-
tions.The complementary gap compares correction
weights across opposite logical outcomes [13, 18, 21,
22]; argument reweighting modifies the error model
and checks agreement between successive logical predic-
tions [12]. Forced gap applies separate logical constraints
forklogical observables usingk+ 1 decoder calls [23].
The third family learns confidence from data.Neu-
ral decoders can support post-selection through per-
logical confidence estimates [24], graph neural network
(GNN) [25] logits used as complementary gap prox-
ies [26], and transformer confidence outputs [27]. Haug
et al. distinguish between low and high noise syn-
dromes [28]. Bhambay et al. use partial syndrome his-
tories for adaptive aborting during syndrome extrac-
tion [29]. In contrast, our models predict the success
of an existing decoder.
Post-selection has also been studied through
statistical-mechanical analyses of abort and condi-
tional logical thresholds [30], and large-deviation
arguments for scalable logical-error suppression [31].
These approaches provide theoretical insights into the
scalability of post-selection.

B. Code families
Our experiments focus on rotated surface code and BB
code memories under circuit-level noise.
Rotated surface codes[32–35]. A planar [[d 2,1, d]]
topological code with weight-4X- andZ-type stabilisers
in the bulk and weight-2 stabilisers at the boundary. We
studyd= 5,7 and 9 instances.
Bivariate bicycle codes[3, 36]. A family of qLDPC
codes constructed from two commuting polynomials over
F2[x, y]/(xl −1, y m −1). They achieve higher encoding
rates and competitive distances to that of the surface
code, but require non-local connectivity. We study the
[[72,12,6]] and [[144,12,12]] instances.
C. Decoders
We benchmark using the following two decoders:
1. Minimum Weight Perfect Matching
The surface code admits a decomposition to agraph-
likeDEM, where each fault results in the flip of at most
two detectors. This property allows it to be decoded
using Minimum Weight Perfect Matching (MWPM). We
use PyMatching [37] as our MWPM implementation.
2. Belief propagation and Localized Statistics Decoding
In general, qLDPC codes do not have graph-like DEMs
and hence we cannot use MWPM; instead, it is common
to use a two-stage general purpose decoder, consisting
of BP [38] and some post-processing step, in our case,
LSD [5]. We provide the full implementation details and
parameters for the decoders in Appendix A 3.
D. Post-selection in quantum error correction
We describe post-selection in the following way. De-
notexfor the information available to a post-selector.
Given a confidence scores(x)∈Rfor each shot, and
thresholdτ∈R, the rule accepts a shot iffs(x)≥τ.
Larger scores indicate greater confidence in decoder suc-
cess. The performance of the post-selector is charac-
terised by two quantities:
Yield(τ) =P(s(x)≥τ),(1)
LER(τ) =P(Ac(σ)̸=Ae|s(x)≥τ),(2)
whereAis the logical action matrix of Section II A. In
practice the yield is the fraction of retained shots and the
conditional LER is defined when the yield is non-zero. A
good post-selector significantly suppresses LER at yields
acceptable to the application.
Existing post-selection strategies.
•Complementary gap [18]:For the surface code, the
complementary gap re-runs the decoder once under
the constraint that the returned correction lies in
the complementary logical class, and uses the log-
likelihood difference between the constrained and
unconstrained corrections, the gap, as the confi-
dence score. A small gap indicates the decoder can-
not confidently distinguish the two outcomes and
such shots are rejected. This construction under-
pins recent surface-code protocols for magic state
preparation [13] and magic state cultivation [22].
The method does not extend directly to high-rate
qLDPC codes as they are not matchable in general
and also have 4 k logical classes, rendering a naive
enumeration of the joint outcomes prohibitively ex-
pensive.

E. Post-selection pipeline
The acceptance thresholdτis calibrated for the de-
sired LER (or yield). For high-rate qLDPC codes, BP
supplies features to the FMPNN which then scores the
shots, and the post-processing (LSD in our case) is exe-
cuted only for accepted shots. For surface codes, given
a syndrome, only one forward pass of the MPNN is re-
quired to produce the score, and accepted shots are then
passed onto MWPM for decoding. The neural networks
do not propose a correction or require repeated decoder
passes, unlike alternative methods. Section III F reports
the costs, with further empirical results in Appendix A 1.

V. CONCLUSIONS, OUTLOOK AND FUTURE
WORK
We introduce a GNN which operates on the native
DEM of a quantum error correcting code, and approaches
the logical error reduction performance of state-of-the-art
techniques, in a single GPU forward pass, without requir-
ing multiple decoder runs. Because training needs only
shots labelled by the deployed decoder, from simulation
or hardware, the approach carries over to other codes and
decoders.
Rare-event sampling:As we pursue larger codes
and/or lower physical error rates, the regime of vanish-
ingly small LERs introduces the well-known rare event
sampling problem where it becomes prohibitively ex-
pensive to collect enough failing shots to train the
MPNN. This is an outstanding challenge across QEC
more broadly, and an active area of research [55–58].
Progress there would allow MPNN training at lower
LERs.
Application to F ault-tolerant procedures:Train-
ing FMPNN models for usage beyond memory in fault-
tolerant protocols which already heavily rely on post-
selection such as distillation [59] or cultivation [22] is an
interesting next step.
Throughput optimisation:In order to avoid the
accumulation of errors during a QEC cycle, one would
like to decode at a throughput of at least one syndrome-
extraction round per microsecond, thus requiring our
post-selector to also fit within these timescales. Promis-
ing work has been done on optimising GNNs for field-

[PDF page 10]
10
programmable gate array (FPGA) application at these
timescales for high energy physics [60], which motivates
further work in this area for post-selection.
Model availability:The MPNN and FMPNN
weights and codebase will shortly be available at
https://github.com/conorcarty/MPNN4QEC.

3. Decoder configurations
For the rotated surface codes, we use MWPM, implemented using the sparse-blossom algorithm inPyMatching[37].
The matching graph is constructed withdecompose errors=True. We use standard matching without correlated
matching enabled. For BB codes (and rotated surface codes post-selected using the clustering approach of [11]), we
use BP+LSD and retain the BP parameters of the original LSD configuration [5]: min-sum BP with a maximum of
30 iterations, with a scaling factor ofα= 0.625, and a parallel update schedule. We use order 0 LSD. We implement
BP+LSD using the soft-output implementation released with [11], built upon theldpcpackage [62]. All qLDPC
training labels and every baseline we report are computed from this configuration.
