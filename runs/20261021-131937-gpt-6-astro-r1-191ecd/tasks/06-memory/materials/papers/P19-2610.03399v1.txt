Optimizing quantum error correction through error attribution
Yuguo Shao1, 2 and Zi-Wen Liu 1
1Yau Mathematical Sciences Center, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084, China
2Yanqi Lake Beijing Institute of Mathematical Sciences and Applications, Beijing 100407, China
Logical error rate is a standard benchmark for quantum error correction, providing an aggregate measure
of the overall effect of physical noise. In actual QEC architectures, errors at different circuit locations can
have markedly different effects on logical failure even when their probabilities are equal. Here we introduce
an error attribution scheme to quantitatively characterize the influence of individual circuit components on the
logical error rate and use this refined information to guide targeted intervention and design for improved QEC
performance. Our attribution estimator computes all component sensitivities jointly from the same Monte Carlo
samples used to estimate the logical error rate. The resulting sensitivity maps identify hotspots where targeted
noise reduction can most effectively improve logical performance. We test the scheme in circuit simulations of
rotated surface code memories and high-rate lifted product codes: halving the noise strength on about 5%–7%
of physical components selected by sensitivity reduces logical error rates by approximately 15%–25%, roughly
twice the mean reduction from the same intervention on an equal number of randomly selected components. By
connecting performance benchmarking to targeted optimization, our scheme provides a practical strategy for
improving quantum error correction with limited resources.
I. INTRODUCTION
Fault-tolerant quantum computation relies on quantum er-
ror correction (QEC) to protect quantum information against
physical noise and support reliable computation [1–4]. Clas-
sical simulation provides a widely used tool to assess the fea-
sibility and performance of candidate QEC schemes by esti-
mating logical error rates under specified noise models [5–
8]. These estimates help determine error thresholds, com-
pare QEC architectures, and quantify resource requirements,
thereby guiding the design of codes, circuits, and decoders for
fault-tolerant computation. However, the logical error rate is
only an aggregate measure that reveals little about the mecha-
nisms underlying logical failure and thus offers limited guid-
ance for improving QEC efficiency.
Physical errors associated with different circuit components
can have different effects on the logical error rate, even when
the component error probabilities are equal. Previous studies
have examined QEC performance under realistic noise [9–12]
and shown that tailoring codes and decoders to noise charac-
teristics can improve performance [13–16]. A complemen-
tary approach is to improve a given QEC scheme through tar-
geted reductions in physical noise. Experimental studies have
used noise models informed by device measurements to esti-
mate the contributions of different error sources (such as two-
qubit gate errors, idle decoherence, readout errors, leakage,
and crosstalk) to logical failure [17, 18]. To assess interven-
tions on individual circuit components, we need to quantify
how reducing each component’s noise would affect the over-
all logical error rate. Efficiently estimating these effects would
help prioritize components for improvement especially when
resources for noise reduction are limited.
In this work, we develop a systematicerror attribution
framework for improving QEC performance by quantitatively
mapping the influence of individual circuit components on
logical failure, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We characterize this
influence through the sensitivity of the logical error rate to
each component’s noise strength for fixed QEC circuits and
decoders. To estimate these sensitivities efficiently, we de-

II. ERROR ATTRIBUTION MAP FRAMEWORK
Attributing the logical error rate to individual noise compo-
nents requires a precise description of the underlying circuit-
level QEC experiment. LetCdenote the circuit implement-
ing a specified memory or logical operation experiment for a
chosen code [2–4]. The circuit specifies the data qubits that
encode the logical state, the ancilla qubits used to measure
checks, and the scheduled sequence of state preparations or
resets, one- and two-qubit gates, idle intervals, and measure-
ments over one or more rounds.
A noisy QEC experiment is specified by the circuitC, a
stochastic error model, and a decoderD. The error model as-
signs parameterized error sources to specific circuit elements
and spacetime locations, each of which we refer to as anoise
component. The measurement record from each run is pro-
cessed to obtain syndrome information and the readout of a
chosen logical observable. The decoderDuses the syndrome
information to infer a correction to this readout. For an ob-
servable with a known ideal outcome, a mismatch between
the corrected readout and the ideal outcome constitutes a log-
ical failure. The overall logical failure rate provides an ag-
gregate measure of the overall effect of physical noise. Our
error attribution framework is designed to quantify and com-
pare the sensitivity of the overall logical error to each compo-
nent’s noise strength microscopically, thus providing a basis
for optimizing QEC performance through informed prioriti-
zation, which is especially important with limited calibration
or design resources.

V . HOTSPOT-GUIDED TARGETED CIRCUIT
INTERVENTION

of targeted therapy: when only a limited number of physical
components can be improved, the intervention can be aimed at
those with the largest sensitivity instead of spread uniformly
over the circuit. We demonstrate the practical benefits of this
strategy in both rotated surface code memories and high-rate
lifted product code instances of intensive interest in practical
QEC.

implementation on e.g. reconfigurable atom array platforms.
VI. DISCUSSION
We introduced the error attribution method that leverages
classical simulation techniques to identify noise bottlenecks
in QEC procedures and guide targeted improvements of their
performance. Our estimator obtains all component sensitiv-
ities from the same Monte Carlo samples used to estimate
the logical error rate, without additional decoder evaluations.
Across the two structurally distinct code families studied here,

[PDF page 7]
7
this information improves target selection under a limited
component budget. The differing hotspot patterns highlight
the importance of assessing intervention priorities for a given
circuit, noise model, and decoder, even when the elementary
error probabilities are uniform.
Future work could extend the framework to account for
both achievable noise reductions and calibration costs when
prioritizing components for intervention. Noise models in-
ferred from device measurements [17, 18] could be combined
with estimates of how available calibration controls change
component error probabilities. Component sensitivities could
then be used to estimate the logical benefit of feasible control
changes and compare that benefit with the resources required.
Further studies could also examine how spatial and temporal
noise correlations affect intervention priorities. Testing and
refining the framework on experimental devices could guide
more effective targeted calibration and accelerate progress to-
ward practical quantum computing.

5. Statistical analysis
We calculate the logical error rates and confidence intervals
using the procedure in Appendix B 2. Within each memory
basis, the logical error rate is the total number of logical fail-
ures divided by the total number of shots. We report the av-
erage of theX- andZ-memory estimates. For random inter-
vention, we then average these results over the40component
sets.
We use50,000bootstrap replicates to quantify the statis-
tical uncertainty. For the simulations without intervention
and after hotspot-guided intervention, each replicate indepen-
dently generates failure counts for each memory basis accord-
ing to Eq. (B4). For random intervention, we draw40values
at random from the logical error rate estimates of the40com-
ponent sets. We average these40sampled estimates to obtain
the logical error rate under random intervention for that boot-
strap replicate. In each replicate, we use the same resampled
baseline rate to calculate the reductions from hotspot-guided
and random intervention. The95%confidence intervals for
the logical error rates and their reductions are given by the
2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap estimates. The
confidence intervals for the improvement ratios are calculated
using the Fieller method in Eq. (B6).
