Stitched surface codes: planar concatenation through GHZ corridors
Mohamed A. Shalby, 1, 2,∗ Alexandre Bourassa, 1 Amir Karamlou, 1 Leigh Martin, 1
Alexis Morvan, 1 Nicholas Noll, 1 Craig Gidney, 1 and Leonid P. Pryadko 1

We present a fault-tolerant quantum-memory architecture that concatenates four distance-tro-
tated surface-code patches with the [[4,2,2]] outer code, encoding two logical qubits at code distance
d= 2tin a physical footprint ofN <2(2t+1) 2 qubits on a square lattice with nearest-neighbor con-
nectivity. The outer-code stabilizer generators XXXXand ZZZZ, each of physical weight 4t, are
measuredin situvia verified Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) ancillae distributed through nar-
row corridors between adjacent patches, without lattice surgery or long-range gates. The outer-code
gadgets introduce hyperedge fault mechanisms, but by design each decomposes into graphlike com-
ponents, so the complete syndrome is decoded by a single minimum-weight-perfect-matching pass.
Circuit-level simulations show logical error rates that scale as∼p t, consistent with distance-2t
protection. Finally, we implement the construction experimentally on a 105-qubit Willow supercon-
ducting processor in both theZZandXXmemory bases. With the correlated-matching decoder,
increasing the code distance fromd= 4 tod= 6 reduces the block logical error rate by a factor
of 1.52. The construction thus provides a near-term testbed for concatenated architectures beyond
the standard surface code.
I. INTRODUCTION
Rotated surface codes are the leading quantum
error-correcting code (QECC) family for superconduct-
ing hardware, owing to their two-dimensional nearest-
neighbor (2D nn) connectivity on a square lattice and
high thresholds [1–3]. In the standard architecture, each
patch of distancedencodes a single logical qubit,k= 1,
inn surf =d 2 data qubits usingd 2 −1 ancillae, for a total
ofN surf = 2d2 −1 physical qubits. Stabilizer generators
are measured by depth-four circuits (counting two-qubit
gates only), and the logical Clifford group is realized, e.g.,
by lattice surgery between adjacent patches [4–7]. Single-
patch surface-code memory has now been realized below
threshold on superconducting hardware, with exponen-
tial suppression up to distance seven [8, 9] (also see ear-
lier distance-three demonstrations on independent plat-
forms [10, 11] and surface-code logical qubits on neutral-
atom arrays [12]).
Beyond surface codes, two large families of fault-
tolerant QECCs are known. First, quantum low-density
parity-check (qLDPC) codes sacrifice the locality in 2D
but may encodek >1 logical qubits per physical block,
in particular, they may have finite asymptotic ratesk/n.
For fault tolerance, it is sufficient that the distance scales
as a logarithm ofnor higher [13, 14]; many families of
such codes are known [15–18], including asymptotically
good families with finite relative distancesd/n[19–21].
Second, concatenated codes, historically the first route
to fault tolerance [22–24], encode each qubit of an outer
code as a logical qubit of an inner code, and also can at-
tain asymptotically constant overhead in a fault-tolerant

setting [25]. Neither construction suits hardware with
limited, fixed qubit connectivity, such as superconducting

A hybrid line of recent work pairs inner surface-code
patches with outer codes to raise the encoding rate with-
out abandoning the inner code’s hardware-friendly struc-
ture. Yoked surface codes [27] measure the stabilizer gen-
erators of a high-rate outer code via lattice surgery to
ancillary “workspace” patches; the qubit savings (nearly
3×in their reported regime) scale with the outer-block
size and only materialize once the outer dimension is
large enough to amortize the workspace overhead. Sim-
ilarly, hierarchical memories [28] concatenate qLDPC
outer codes with surface-code inner blocks via surgery-
style measurements. Unfortunately, these constructions,
designed for the large-scale fault-tolerant regime, require
too many physical qubits to demonstrate non-trivial dis-
tance scaling and/or an advantage over surface codes on
existing or near-term superconducting devices [27, 28].

The GHZ corridors introduce a potential structural
complication for decoding. A single circuit-level fault
inside the corridor can simultaneously trigger detec-
tors in the corridor itself and in two or more adjacent
patches, producing hyperedge fault mechanisms in the
detector error model [32]. Decoders that natively han-
dle hyperedges—e.g., belief propagation with ordered
statistics (BP+OSD) [33, 34] and search-based decoders
such as Tesseract [35]—can handle these mechanisms
more accurately than minimum-weight perfect matching
(MWPM), but at substantially greater runtime, which
limits their use at scale and at real-time hardware cy-
cle times. Here we construct the corridor checks and
verifiers to ensure that every such mechanism decom-
poses into graphlike components, so that the complete
syndrome, including the outer-code detectors, is decoded
by a single MWPM pass [36], optionally with correlated
reweighting [37].
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section II reviews the relevant background: surface-code
preliminaries, the concatenation framework, the [[4,2,2]]
outer code, and standard schemes for outer-stabilizer
measurement. Section III specifies our architecture: the
four-patch layout, the GHZ corridor preparation and ver-
ification protocol, and the scheduling that interleaves
outer-code rounds with inner-patch cycles. Section IV
describes how the complete syndrome is decoded by
matching. Section V presents circuit-level simulation re-
sults under the SI1000 error model [38], including the
verification-depth tradeoff, distance scaling, and foot-

mentation on a 105-qubit superconducting Willow pro-
cessor in two memory bases. Specifically, we implement

tion VII closes with the discussion of geometric and mod-

problems. Additional theoretical and experimental de-
tails are presented in the Appendices.
II. PRELIMINARIES
A. Rotated surface codes
The rotated surface code of distancetencodes one log-
ical qubit inn=t 2 data qubits, orN surf = 2t2 −1 phys-
ical qubits including syndrome ancillae, arranged on a
square lattice with open boundaries [4, 7, 39]. The code
is a CSS code whose stabilizer generators are weight-four
X-type plaquettes and weight-fourZ-type plaquettes in
the bulk, together with weight-two operators on opposite
pairs of boundaries, see Fig. 1 (left). Minimal-weight log-
ical operators Xand Zare strings of weighttconnect-
ing the two pairs of boundaries. A single round of syn-
drome extraction interleaves theX- andZ-type measure-
ments in a depth-four circuit (depth-six if basis-change
layers are counted) between data and adjacent syndrome
qubits [1, 2].
B. Concatenated quantum codes
The concatenation of an outer codeQ o = [[no, ko, do]]
with an inner codeQ i = [[n i, ki, di]] encodes each qubit
ofQ o as a logical qubit ofQ i [23, 31], which gives a code
Qo ⋆Q i with parameters
[[noni, k oki, d]], d≥d o di.(1)
The distance-product bound is due to the fact that an
outer logical operator must have support on at leastd o
inner blocks, and each nontrivial inner logical represen-
tative has weight at leastd i. For stochastic circuit-level
noise this code-distance statement translates into logical-
failure scaling governed by the minimum number of phys-
ical faults needed to realize such a representative.

D. Outer-stabilizer measurement
Three standard schemes measure a weight-wstabi-
lizer generator fault-tolerantly with the help of ancillary
qubits: Shor-style cat ancilla [22, 41], Steane-style en-
coded ancilla [23, 42], and Knill-style ancilla teleporta-
tion [31]. All three reduce the syndrome-extraction cir-
cuit to transversal CNOT gates between an ancilla reg-
ister and the data qubits supporting the operator being
measured. Steane and Knill schemes scale ancilla over-
head with the size of an encoded block and are well-suited
when the data block is itself encoded. Shor-style cat an-
cillae use a single GHZ-type resource state and incur the
lowest overhead. For weight-wstabilizer measurement, a
Shor-style scheme prepares aw-qubit GHZ state

conducting setting and to repeated extraction; its Pauli
corrections are absorbed into the detector frame rather
than applied by feedforward.
III. ARCHITECTURE
A. Circuit layout and qubit count
The full syndrome extraction circuit is parameterized
by the inner-code distancet, the number of GHZ-state
verification roundsr v, the number of full outer-code syn-
drome measurement roundsr o, and the circuit basis (X
orZ). Figure 1 shows the stabilizer structure of our
construction att= 3: four rotated surface-code patches
occupy the four quadrants of a square arrangement, with
N= 93 physical qubits,k= 2 logical qubits, and code
distanced= 2t= 6. Two overlapping narrow ancillacor-
ridorsof length 4tthread between the four patches. They
are used to sequentially measure the outer-code stabilizer
generatorsG X = XXXXandG Z = ZZZZ. Each cor-
ridor carries a 4t-qubit GHZ resource state, and the cor-
ridor qubits next to the patches also serve as boundary
measure qubits of the patches (Sec. III C).
In aZ-basis circuit, the initial reset is followed by a
single round of inner-codes’ syndrome measurements and
aG X measurement. This preparation round is followed
byr o outer-code rounds, each including oneG Z and one
GX measurement, followed by a final measurement of all
data qubits in theZbasis. Each outer-code generator
measurement consists of a single preparation round and
rv verification rounds, each of the same depth and done
concurrently with a round of the inner-code syndrome
measurement, see Figs. 2 and 3. Thus, preparation round
ends atτ 0 =r v + 2, and the subsequent outer-code gen-
erators are measured atτ 1 = 2rv + 3,τ 2 = 3rv + 4, etc.,
with intervals ofr v + 1. The firstZ-detector is placed at
τ1, and the firstX-detector atτ 2; subsequent detectors
are placed after each outer-code cycle of duration 2rv +2.
TheX-basis circuits are constructed similarly, up to
the basis for initial preparation / final measurements,
and the order of outer-code generator measurements.
Namely, after the initial round of surface code measure-
ments, inner-code measurements are combined withG Z,
GX,G Z, etc., done in this order, with the detectors
placed atτ 1,τ 2, etc. While the two circuits are not iden-
tical, they have the same repeat blocks in the bulk. This
choice is made to reduce the initial state preparation and
measurement (SPAM) errors. Indeed, if we were to mea-
sureG X first in theX-basis circuit, the first complete
set of outer-code detectors would result only atτ 2.

2t2−1 physical qubits each with the 8t+1 shared ancillary
qubits required to measure the outer stabilizer generators
(Sec. III C) gives the physical footprint

B. GHZ corridor preparation and verification
A single GHZ preparation round (Fig. 2) constructs the
resource state|GHZ 4t⟩on the 4tcorridor qubits using
mid-circuit measurements [46]. The protocol prepares
clusters of corridor qubits in smaller GHZ states, mea-
sures intermediate qubits in the appropriate fusion bases
to form a GHZ line, and absorbs the resulting Pauli cor-
rections virtually as detector-frame updates. The prepa-
ration circuit has constant depth, independent oft.
Preparation alone is not fault-tolerant: a single fault
during preparation can leave a multi-qubitXerror on
the corridor, which would propagate as a hook error of
weight greater than one through the subsequent transver-
sal CNOTs into data. We follow each preparation byr v
rounds of verification. A verification round (Fig. 3) mea-
sures pairwiseZZparities along the corridor by short-
depth CNOTs into the ancillae on the corridor’s center
line;Xerrors that arose during preparation or earlier
verification rounds flip these parities, whileZerrors com-
mute through the subsequent transversal CNOTs and
contribute only to the readout. On the Z-corridor the
roles ofXandZare exchanged: the verification rounds
measure pairwiseXXparities, which detect theZerrors
that would propagate into the data.
Afterr v verification rounds, the GHZ ancilla interacts
transversally with the supporting data qubits of the four
patches: CNOTs from corridor to data for the X-corridor

C. Reuse of boundary ancillae and scheduling
The corridor qubits adjacent to each patch’s boundary
serve a dual role. During the surface-code stabilizer cycle
they participate as weight-two boundary syndrome ancil-
lae for the inner patch; during the corridor cycle they
participate as edge qubits of the GHZ resource state.
We schedule the two roles so that boundary stabilizer
generators are temporarily disabled during the rounds
in which the corresponding ancilla qubits are committed
to the corridor, and re-enabled afterward. This dual-
use scheduling avoids a separate set of boundary ancillae
along the corridors and keeps the total ancilla overhead
at 8t+ 1.
Overall, each outer-generator measurement occupies
rv+1 inner cycles, which includes GHZ-state preparation,
ther v verification rounds, transversal extraction, and the
corridor readout. A full outer-code measurement cycle—
one round each ofG X andG Z measurements—occupies
2(rv + 1) inner-code cycles.
IV. DECODING
The decoder operates on the circuit-level detector error
model (DEM) extracted by Stim [32] and uses minimum-
weight perfect matching (MWPM) [2, 36], which requires
every fault mechanism to flip at most two detectors or to
decompose into components that do.
The GHZ corridors add fault mechanisms that flip
more than two detectors. A fault on a corridor qubit dur-
ing transversal extraction can spread onto the data qubits
of adjacent patches; it then flips detectors in the corri-
dor and in several patches, and, when the spread reaches
a logical representative, a logical observable. The corri-
dor checks and verifier detectors are declared such that
Stim decomposes every such mechanism into components
that each flip at most two detectors. The GHZ prepara-
tion outcomes define known Pauli frames on the corridor
(Sec. III B); rather than being applied as measurement-
conditioned corrections, these frame bits are included di-
rectly in the detector declarations. The resulting DEM
is decoded by a single PyMatching call over all four
patches [36, 48]. The outer-code detectors are nodes of
the same matching graph, so error chains that cross patch
boundaries are matched through them, and no separate
outer-code decoding step is needed. In the early stages
of this work, before the circuits were made matchable,
we validated them with the search-based decoder Tesser-
act [35], which is slow for large distances.
Decomposition assigns independent weights to the
components of a mechanism and therefore ignores their
correlation. Correlated matching in PyMatching (avail-
able since v2.3 [48]) uses these correlations to reweight
the matching graph after a first pass and then matches
again [37]. We report both plain MWPM and corre-
lated matching (Corr-MWPM) for the hardware data in
Sec. VI.

other outer stabilizer. The optimalr v therefore depends

In the experiment we executed the full stitched-code

the below-threshold surface-code memory experiments of
Ref. [9], using reinforcement-learning-based calibration

circuits apply data-qubit leakage removal (DQLR) [50]

The stitched-code layout differs in one important re-
spect from the native layout used in standard rotated
surface-code memory experiments [1, 9]. In the native
layout, data and syndrome qubits form a bipartite pat-
tern: the syndrome-qubit sublattice is optimized for re-
peated measurement and reset, while the data-qubit sub-
lattice is optimized for coherent storage followed by a sin-
gle final readout [9]. In the stitched circuits, by contrast,
some qubits that would ordinarily serve as data qubits
are repurposed as corridor ancillae and must therefore be
measured and reset in every outer-code extraction round.
This makes the hardware implementation more demand-
ing than a native surface-code memory experiment: al-
though the circuit uses the same processor primitives, it
requires repeated measurement and reset on qubits that
are not optimized for these operations.

C. Logical error probabilities per cycle
For quantum memory based on surface codes, a de
facto standard measure used to compare experiments of
different duration is the average logical error probability
per cycle,ε[9, 51].
With two logical qubits, there are three related prob-
abilities (per surface-code cycle) that can be computed
for each experiment: the marginal error probability in
each logical channel,ε 0 andε 1, and the block error prob-
ability,ε L. Since we use the duration of the surface code
cycle as the time unit, to linear order in small error rates,
these probabilities are numerically close to the respective
marginal error ratesµ 0 =µ 01 +µ 11,µ 1 =µ 10 +µ 11, and
the total error rateµ tot =µ 01 +µ 10 +µ 11. The full ex-
pressions for the three probabilities are given in Eq. (B6).
We used these equations to compute per-cycle logical
error probabilities. The resulting basis-averaged quanti-
ties are shown in Table II, along with the corresponding
improvement factors Λ 4→6 ≡ε (d=4)/ε(d=6). In particu-
lar, for plain MWPM, increasing the code distance from
d= 4 tod= 6 reduces the block error probability per
cycleε L from 6.845(15)% to 4.209(8)%, corresponding
to an improvement factor Λ 4→6 = 1.626(5). Correlated
matching lowers the error probability by 15% atd= 4,
to 5.836(12)%, and by 9% atd= 6, to 3.850(7)%, which
results in a smaller Λ 4→6 = 1.516(4).
D. Differences with surface code experiments
One immediate observation from the experimental
plots in Figs. 9 and 10 is that theX-basis circuits have
higher logical error rates than theZ-basis circuits, with
the difference most pronounced atd= 4. This is con-
firmed by the per-basis fitted rates listed in Table I.
Given that theX- andZ-basis circuits are nearly iden-
tical, we explain this asymmetry by low-frequency phase
noise which cannot be entirely eliminated by the device
calibration. We mention in passing that phase noise was
also present in the surface-code memory experiment done
on a similar Willow device [9]. The basis asymmetry was
suppressed by using anXZZXversion of the surface
codes [52], unlike the CSS codes we use in this work.
Another important observation is that the outer-code
detectors, even though they are not used frequently, are
absolutely essential to achieve the small error rates for
stitched codes. These detectors show much higher firing
rates than the regular surface-code detectors [see Fig. 13
and Appendix D 1], as would be expected from the large
physical weight 4tof the outer-code stabilizer generators.
Indeed, in thed= 6 hardware experiment they fire with
probability close to 1/2, yet flipping the value of any one
of the primary-basis outer-code detectors changes a de-
coded logical in about 80% of the shots, and in these shots
it is the measured value, rather than its flip, that gives
the correct logical outcome 95% of the time (as computed
for the experiment withr o = 1, where each outer-code
detector is present only once, see Appendix D 2).
The high firing rate of the outer-code detectors also
delimits the role of the corridor GHZ gadgets. A mem-
ory experiment can afford such a rate: the outer code
only has to catch the rare logical failures of individual
patches, and it has no deadline, so each further outer-
code round makes such a failure easier to tell from a mis-
read. In contrast, a parity measurement between patches
must settle the eigenvalue of an outer-code generator by
a given time. Here, one outer-code round per 2(r v + 1)
surface-code cycles is way too slow for most applications.
Such a sparsity of undetectable time-like outer-code er-

VII. OUTLOOK AND CONCLUSION
We have introduced a stitched concatenated memory
that combines four rotated surface-code patches with
the [[4,2,2]] outer detection code in a strictly planar
nearest-neighbor layout. The construction encodes two
logical qubits at code distanced= 2twhile using a
physical footprintN= 2d 2 + 4d−3, and its verified
GHZ corridors measure the two weight-4touter stabi-
lizer generators without lattice surgery, long-range gates,
or workspace patches. Circuit-level simulations show the
expected distance-2tscaling (Figs. 5 and 11), and the
hardware experiment on a 105-qubit Willow processor
shows that the same architecture can be executed on a
present superconducting device, with the logical error per
cycle decreasing fromd= 4 tod= 6 in both memory
bases (Table II).
The main practical tradeoff is the corridor prefactor:
the asymptotic factor-of-two gain in qubits per logical
qubit is realized below a crossover physical error rate
that lies within reach of near-term superconducting hard-
ware. The stitched layout also packs both logical qubits
into a single, nearly square region, whereas two indepen-
dent patches of comparable distance form a rectangle. In
situations where availability, processor tiles, packaging,
control wiring, or modular interconnects favor square re-
gions, this geometry is an advantage independent of qubit
count.
Several directions remain open. Hardware-specific op-
timization of the corridor readout/reset path, leakage-
removal scheduling, and calibration of the dual-use
boundary ancillae should reduce the corridor prefactor.
More broadly, the GHZ-corridor gadget is not specific to
the [[4,2,2]] code: it suggests a way to realize other small
outer codes whose stabilizer generators can be routed
through planar corridors. Identifying such codes, com-
paring their layout and decoder tradeoffs, and developing
logical operations on and between stitched blocks given
their slower accumulation of time-like distance, are natu-
ral next steps toward using compact concatenated mod-
ules as building blocks for larger fault-tolerant architec-
tures.

Appendix D: Detector-level diagnostics
This appendix collects diagnostics of the hardware analysis: finite-duration single-point checks of the per-cycle
rates, a comparison of detector statistics with a noisy circuit-level simulation, and the decoding role of the outer-code
detectors.
1. Detector probabilities and correlations
Figures 13–15 compare thed= 6,r o = 6ZZhardware data with a simulation of the same executed circuit,
including its single-qubit echo pulses, each withN shot = 200,000 shots. The simulation uses a distributed noise model
built from the per-qubit and per-pair calibration of the device (Fig. 8): each single-qubit gate and CZ gate is followed
by depolarizing noise with its measured Pauli error, each measurement outcome is flipped with the measured readout
error of its qubit, and each idle qubit receives Pauli-twirled amplitude and phase damping set by itsT 1 andT ϕ over
the duration of the layer. Layer durations follow Ref. [9]. The simulation reproduces the grouped structure of the
detection-event fractions, including the saturated outer-code detectors and the periodic peaks on the corridor-adjacent
patch detectors [Fig. 13]. Across all 1,384 detectors, the hardware and simulated fire probabilities have a Pearson
correlation of 0.93, with means of 8.0% and 6.3%, respectively [Fig. 14, left]. For detector pairs, we treat a squared
Pearson correlationR 2 as resolved when it exceeds 25/N shot, that is, when the correlation exceeds five standard
deviations of its shot noise. Of the 11,455 pairs resolved in either data set, 6,505 are resolved in both, and their logR 2
values have a correlation of 0.89 [Fig. 14, right]. Of the others, 4,522 are resolved only in the hardware data and 428
only in the simulation; together with the lower simulated mean fire probability, this indicates hardware noise, such as
leakage and crosstalk, beyond the calibrated gate, readout, and idle errors. The full correlation matrices in Fig. 15
show the same local structure in both data sets.
2. Decoding role of the outer-code detectors
Atd= 6 every outer-code detector fires with probability within about 0.01 of 1/2 [Fig. 13, left], so in isolation it
cannot be distinguished from a random bit. Its value to the decoder is conditional on the rest of the syndrome. To
measure it, we decode every shot twice with correlated matching: once with the measured value of a given outer-code
detector and once with that value flipped, keeping all other detectors fixed. We call a shot decisive for the detector
when the two decoded logicals differ.

the measured value gives the correct logical outcome in 82.7% of cases and the flipped value in 4.7%, so whenever
exactly one of the two is correct, the measured value is the correct one 95% of the time. TheXXdata give 78%
decisive shots and 93% for the same ratio. Replacing the outer-code detectors by independent random bits with the

A memory experiment tests the ability to move logical ob-
servables through time; a stability experiment [53] tests the
ability to move them through space, that is, to determine
the value of a product of stabilizer generators, as needed, for
example, by measurement-based logical operations such as
lattice surgery [4, 5, 7]. Following Ref. [53], we construct a
stability experiment for the outer-code generatorG X of the
stitched code (Fig. 16, left): the patches haveX-type bound-
aries on the three sides that do not face the X-corridor, so
that theirX-type generators multiply toG X. To keep the
circuit faithful,G Z and theZ-type generators are still mea-
sured, although the observable does not depend on them.
SinceG Z may flip fourX-type generators, one per patch, by a common random sign, we track these generators in
pairs at the two ends of the Z-corridor, so that every fault mechanism decomposes into graphlike components. The
time-like distance is then the number ofG X outcomes,d time =r o + 1 (Table IV).
We compare it with the surface-code stability circuit of Ref. [53] at distance 2t, using SI1000 noise [38] and Corr-
MWPM decoding for both. Fort= 3, each outer-code round lowers the failure probabilityp F,stab by a factor of
1.5, 2.0, and 4.5 atp= 10 −3, 5×10 −4, and 10 −4 (Fig. 16, middle): the outer code is fault tolerant in time. This
protection comes at a cost in time, since one unit ofd time takes 2(r v + 1) surface-code cycles instead of one. At
p= 10 −3, reachingp F,stab = 10−3 takes about 80 cycles, compared with about 5 for the surface code (Fig. 16, right).
Since the optimalr v depends ontandp(Sec. V B), the number of cycles needed to reach a givenp F,stab requires a
careful analysis in each case, but in all cases we studied (t= 3, 5) it is much larger than for the surface code.
Thus GHZ corridors serve memory well, since only changes of the outer-code generators matter there, but any logical
operation that relies on corridor outcomes inherits their slow accumulation of time-like distance, and we do not expect
GHZ corridors to be a competitive replacement for lattice surgery in computation. Logical operations on stitched
codes can instead avoid relying on corridor outcomes, for example through lattice surgery on the patch boundaries
that do not face the corridors, or through code automorphisms [54–56], such as the physical-qubit permutations of
the [[4,2,2]] code (Sec. II C). We leave logical operations on stitched codes, and stitched codes with other small outer
codes, to future work.
